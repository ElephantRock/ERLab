## Abstract

The rapid proliferation of Graph-of-Thought (GoT) frameworks has fundamentally advanced the multi-step reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). However, the selection of optimal reasoning structures—ranging from linear Chain-of-Thought (CoT) to complex directed acyclic graphs—currently relies on empirical trial-and-error, lacking a rigorous mathematical foundation. This proposal introduces TopoReason, a formal complexity-theoretic framework that bridges LLM token generation mechanics with formal graph theory and Kolmogorov complexity to predict and bound the performance of structural reasoning topologies. By formally defining Reasoning Topologies as distinct computational automata, TopoReason derives theoretical bounds on the error probability and token complexity for varying graph structures. Furthermore, it introduces a topology selection algorithm that analyzes a prompt's latent dependency graph to map queries to the minimal reasoning topology required to guarantee a bounded error rate. The expected result is a unified theoretical foundation that transforms structural LLM reasoning from a heuristic engineering trick into a predictable, cost-efficient, and mathematically grounded discipline. Empirical validation across standard multi-step reasoning benchmarks will demonstrate the framework's ability to accurately predict the trade-offs between reasoning depth, breadth, token consumption, and overall accuracy.

## Introduction

The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) has ushered in a new era of artificial intelligence, demonstrating unprecedented capabilities in natural language understanding, generation, and complex problem-solving. Among the most significant advancements has been the development of structured reasoning frameworks, which prompt LLMs to break down complex queries into manageable intermediate steps. Beginning with linear Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, the field has progressively evolved toward more sophisticated structural paradigms. Frameworks such as Tree-of-Thoughts (ToT) and Graph-of-Thoughts (GoT) have demonstrated that allowing an LLM to explore multiple reasoning paths, backtrack from errors, and merge disparate insights can yield substantial improvements on highly complex tasks, including creative writing, mathematical reasoning, and combinatorial optimization (Besta et al., 2024). These structural approaches mirror human cognitive processes, where problem-solving rarely follows a strictly linear path but instead involves dynamic exploration, revision, and synthesis of ideas. As LLMs are increasingly deployed in high-stakes domains such as biomedicine, autonomous driving, and legal analysis, the ability to robustly and reliably structure their internal reasoning processes becomes not just an academic curiosity, but a critical necessity for ensuring safety, reliability, and interpretability.

Despite the empirical success of these advanced reasoning frameworks, the field currently suffers from a profound theoretical deficit. The transition from CoT to ToT and GoT has been driven largely by intuitive heuristics and empirical engineering rather than formal mathematical principles. When presented with a new, unseen reasoning task, practitioners have no rigorous methodology to determine whether the problem requires the simplicity of a linear chain, the exploratory breadth of a tree, or the complex, intertwined dependencies of a general graph. Current state-of-the-art approaches, such as RouteGoT and Adaptive Graph of Thoughts, attempt to address this by using reinforcement learning or heuristic routing to dynamically select the reasoning structure at runtime. However, these methods essentially treat the symptom rather than the underlying disease; they rely on costly trial-and-error during inference and provide no *a priori* mathematical guarantees on the computational complexity or reasoning fidelity of the chosen topology. This lack of a unified theoretical foundation forces an inefficient, brute-force approach to prompt engineering, leading to sub-optimal trade-offs between accuracy, latency, and token consumption. Without a formal understanding of *why* and *when* a specific graph topology outperforms others, the design of structural reasoning frameworks will remain an unpredictable black art, hindering the development of truly efficient and reliable LLM reasoning systems.

To address this fundamental gap, this proposal introduces TopoReason, a formal complexity-theoretic framework designed to provide a rigorous mathematical foundation for optimal reasoning topology selection. The core innovation of TopoReason lies in its formalization of reasoning topologies as distinct computational automata and its derivation of theoretical bounds on error probability and token complexity based on the intrinsic properties of the target reasoning task. By analyzing the latent dependency graph of a given prompt—extracted via an auxiliary lightweight LLM—TopoReason maps the problem's structural requirements to the minimal reasoning topology required to guarantee a bounded error rate. This approach shifts the paradigm of structural LLM reasoning from empirical observation to predictable computer science, formally quantifying the trade-offs between reasoning depth, breadth, and token consumption. 

The expected contributions of this research are threefold. First, it will establish the first unified theoretical foundation for structural LLM reasoning, formally defining the computational power and limitations of CoT, ToT, and GoT. Second, it will provide mathematically proven guarantees on when complex graph structures structurally outperform simpler linear or tree-based approaches. Third, it will deliver a practical, computationally efficient topology selection algorithm that optimizes for both accuracy and cost. The subsequent sections of this proposal will detail the related work, the formal mathematical definitions and algorithmic steps of the TopoReason framework, a comprehensive evaluation plan, and a realistic timeline for execution.

## Related Work

**Graph-Based Reasoning Frameworks:** The evolution of structured reasoning in LLMs has progressed rapidly from linear to complex graph topologies. Besta et al. (2024) introduced the Graph of Thoughts (GoT) framework, which advances prompting capabilities by modeling the reasoning process as a directed graph. This allows LLMs to combine arbitrary thoughts, loop back to previous states, and aggregate information in ways that linear Chain-of-Thought (CoT) or Tree-of-Thoughts (ToT) cannot. However, GoT treats the graph structure as a user-defined parameter, offering no theoretical guidance on *when* a general graph is strictly necessary versus a simpler tree or chain. Similarly, research on effective graph-of-thought reasoning in language models has demonstrated empirical improvements in NLP tasks by leveraging network structures, but these approaches largely rely on static, pre-defined graph schemas that cannot adapt to the varying structural demands of diverse reasoning tasks. The limitation across these foundational frameworks is the reliance on human intuition to design the reasoning topology, which introduces inefficiencies and prevents the formalization of performance guarantees.

**Adaptive and Routing-Based Approaches:** Recognizing the inefficiencies of static topologies, recent work has focused on dynamic adaptation. RouteGoT proposes node-adaptive routing for cost-efficient GoT reasoning, using learned policies to decide whether to expand a reasoning node linearly or as a branching graph structure. While this reduces computational waste, it operates as a heuristic runtime patch rather than a theoretically grounded solution. The routing decisions are based on observed intermediate states rather than formal complexity bounds, meaning the system must incur the cost of generating initial thoughts before it can optimize the topology. Similarly, Adaptive Graph of Thoughts attempts test-time adaptive reasoning by unifying chain, tree, and graph structures. It dynamically adjusts the topology based on the perceived difficulty of the prompt. The critical limitation of these adaptive methods is their reliance on empirical proxies for "difficulty" rather than formal measures of computational complexity. They provide no *a priori* guarantees on token consumption or error rates, and their routing mechanisms add an additional layer of computational overhead that lacks theoretical justification.

**Cognitive and Theoretical Foundations of External Representations:** The theoretical underpinnings of how external representations aid problem-solving have long been studied in cognitive science. Zhang (1997) proposed a theoretical framework for external representation-based problem solving, demonstrating that the structure of an external representation (like a graph or a list) directly determines the cognitive complexity of the task. While this work provides a strong cognitive motivation for TopoReason, it predates modern LLMs and does not account for the specific mechanics of autoregressive token generation, such as attention dilution and context window limitations. Furthermore, Pearl’s foundational work on causality (Pearl, 2010) provides robust models for causal inference, but there remains a distinct methodological gap in how to ground the correlational token-generation of LLMs in strict causal logic. Current LLM reasoning is fundamentally associative, and existing theoretical models do not bridge the gap between the sequential nature of LLM inference and the complex, non-linear dependency graphs required for rigorous logical deduction. TopoReason addresses this by explicitly mapping the latent dependencies of a problem to the computational automata that govern LLM reasoning.

## Proposed Method

The TopoReason framework is built upon a formalization of structural LLM reasoning that bridges the mechanics of autoregressive token generation with computational complexity theory. The core intuition is that any multi-step reasoning problem possesses an intrinsic *Latent Dependency Graph* (LDG), and the topology required to solve it with bounded error is determined by the structural properties of this LDG. We formalize this intuition through three key components: (1) the formal definition of Reasoning Topologies as computational automata, (2) the derivation of theoretical bounds on error probability and token complexity, and (3) an algorithmic approach to optimal topology selection.

**Formal Problem Definition:**
Let a reasoning problem be defined as a tuple $P = (Q, A, \mathcal{D})$, where $Q$ is the natural language query, $A$ is the target answer, and $\mathcal{D}$ is the latent dependency graph representing the logical dependencies between intermediate reasoning steps. The nodes $v \in \mathcal{V}(\mathcal{D})$ represent atomic logical operations or facts, and the edges $e \in \mathcal{E}(\mathcal{D})$ represent strict logical dependencies where $e = (v_i, v_j)$ implies that the operation $v_j$ requires the output of $v_i$.

A Reasoning Topology $\mathcal{T}$ is defined as a computational automaton $\mathcal{A}_\mathcal{T}$ that governs the generation of intermediate tokens. We define a hierarchy of topologies based on their structural constraints:
1.  **Chain ($\mathcal{T}_{chain}$):** A deterministic finite automaton where the state transition is strictly linear, $\mathcal{V} = \{v_1, v_2, ..., v_n\}$ and $\mathcal{E} = \{(v_i, v_{i+1})\}$. The maximum indegree and outdegree are bounded by 1.
2.  **Tree ($\mathcal{T}_{tree}$):** A non-deterministic automaton allowing branching. The maximum indegree is 1, but the outdegree can be $>1$, enabling exploration of multiple reasoning paths.
3.  **DAG ($\mathcal{T}_{dag}$):** A directed acyclic graph where indegree can be $>1$, allowing the aggregation of multiple distinct reasoning paths, but disallowing cycles.
4.  **General Graph ($\mathcal{T}_{graph}$):** A general automaton allowing cycles, enabling iterative refinement and feedback loops.

The objective of TopoReason is to find the optimal topology $\mathcal{T}^*$ that minimizes the expected error probability $\mathbb{E}[P_{error}]$ subject to a token budget $B$:
$$ \mathcal{T}^* = \arg\min_{\mathcal{T} \in \mathfrak{T}} \mathbb{E}[P_{error}(\mathcal{T}, \mathcal{D})] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbb{E}[C_{token}(\mathcal{T}, \mathcal{D})] \leq B $$
where $\mathfrak{T} = \{\mathcal{T}_{chain}, \mathcal{T}_{tree}, \mathcal{T}_{dag}, \mathcal{T}_{graph}\}$ is the set of permissible reasoning topologies.

**Derivation of Theoretical Bounds:**
We model the error probability of a single reasoning step $v_i$ as a function of its dependency depth $d(v_i)$ and the number of active dependencies (indegree) $k_i$. Let the base probability of an LLM generating an incorrect token for an atomic operation be $p_0$. For a linear chain, errors compound strictly sequentially. The probability of a successful reasoning path of length $L$ is:
$$ P_{success}(\mathcal{T}_{chain}) = (1 - p_0)^L $$
For a tree topology with branching factor $b$, the system explores $b$ independent paths. If the aggregation mechanism (e.g., majority voting) requires $m$ correct paths out of $b$, the success probability improves, but the token cost increases exponentially. For a DAG, the error probability of a node $v_j$ with indegree $k_j$ is modeled as:
$$ P_{error}(v_j) = 1 - (1 - p_0) \prod_{i=1}^{k_j} (1 - P_{error}(v_i)) $$
This formulation captures the compounding risk of dependency aggregation. The token complexity $C_{token}$ is derived from the number of nodes and edges in the reasoning graph. For a DAG, $C_{token}(\mathcal{T}_{dag}) = O(|\mathcal{V}| + |\mathcal{E}|)$, while for a tree with depth $L$ and branching factor $b$, $C_{token}(\mathcal{T}_{tree}) = O(b^L)$. By analyzing the Kolmogorov complexity $K(\mathcal{D})$ of the latent dependency graph, we can bound the minimum token cost required to faithfully represent the problem structure.

**Topology Selection Algorithm:**
The TopoReason selection algorithm operates in two phases:
1.  **Dependency Extraction:** Given a query $Q$, an auxiliary lightweight LLM (e.g., Llama-3-8B) is prompted to generate the latent dependency graph $\hat{\mathcal{D}}$. This is achieved by instructing the model to identify the key logical operations and their prerequisite relationships in a structured JSON format.
2.  **Complexity Mapping:** The algorithm computes the structural properties of $\hat{\mathcal{D}}$, specifically its maximum indegree $\Delta^{-}(\hat{\mathcal{D}})$, maximum outdegree $\Delta^{+}(\hat{\mathcal{D}})$, and whether it contains cycles. The optimal topology is selected based on the following decision boundary:
    *   If $\Delta^{-}(\hat{\mathcal{D}}) = 1$ and $\Delta^{+}(\hat{\mathcal{D}}) = 1$, select $\mathcal{T}_{chain}$.
    *   If $\Delta^{-}(\hat{\mathcal{D}}) = 1$ and $\Delta^{+}(\hat{\mathcal{D}}) > 1$, select $\mathcal{T}_{tree}$.
    *   If $\Delta^{-}(\hat{\mathcal{D}}) > 1$ and $\hat{\mathcal{D}}$ is acyclic, select $\mathcal{T}_{dag}$.
    *   If $\hat{\mathcal{D}}$ contains cycles, select $\mathcal{T}_{graph}$.

This algorithm minimizes the structural mismatch between the problem's intrinsic dependencies and the reasoning topology, ensuring that the token budget is utilized efficiently without sacrificing reasoning fidelity.

## Expected Contributions

1.  **Unified Theoretical Foundation for Structural LLM Reasoning:** This work will establish the first formal, mathematical framework defining Reasoning Topologies as distinct computational automata. This contribution matters because it transforms structural LLM reasoning from a collection of empirical prompting tricks into a rigorous computer science discipline, enabling formal analysis of computational limits and capabilities.
2.  **Formally Proven Bounds on Error and Token Complexity:** The research will deliver mathematically derived bounds linking the structural properties of a problem's dependency graph (indegree, outdegree, depth) to the expected error probability and token consumption of different topologies. This is critical for providing practitioners with *a priori* guarantees on the cost and accuracy of LLM reasoning, moving the field away from costly trial-and-error.
3.  **Practical and Efficient Topology Selection Algorithm:** Beyond theory, this work contributes a functional, lightweight algorithm for predicting the optimal reasoning topology (Chain, Tree, DAG, Graph) for any given prompt. This algorithm allows developers to automatically optimize inference pipelines for both speed and accuracy, significantly reducing the computational overhead and latency associated with complex LLM reasoning tasks.
4.  **Empirical Validation of Complexity-Theoretic Predictions:** By comparing the theoretical predictions of TopoReason against empirical results on standard benchmarks, this research will provide the first large-scale validation of complexity-theoretic models in the context of LLM structural reasoning, offering invaluable data on the alignment between theoretical automata and stochastic LLM behavior.

## Evaluation Plan

**Datasets**
The evaluation will utilize four widely recognized benchmarks spanning different domains and reasoning complexities:
1.  **GSM8K** (Grade School Math 8K): Contains 8,500 grade school math word problems requiring multi-step arithmetic reasoning. Source: https://huggingface.co/datasets/gsm8k. This dataset primarily tests deep linear reasoning, making it ideal for evaluating scenarios where $\mathcal{T}_{chain}$ should theoretically suffice.
2.  **MMLU** (Massive Multitask Language Understanding): Comprises 14,042 multiple-choice questions across 57 domains, including abstract algebra and law. Source: https://huggingface.co/datasets/cais/mmlu. This dataset tests broad knowledge retrieval and complex cross-domain dependencies, suitable for evaluating $\mathcal{T}_{tree}$ and $\mathcal{T}_{dag}$.
3.  **ARC-Challenge** (AI2 Reasoning Challenge): Contains 2,590 grade-school science questions requiring complex logical deduction. Source: https://huggingface.co/datasets/allenai/ai2_arc. This dataset is used to evaluate scenarios requiring high reasoning fidelity and complex dependency resolution.
4.  **StrategyQA**: Consists of 2,780 yes/no questions requiring multi-hop implicit reasoning. Source: https://huggingface.co/datasets/strategyqa. This dataset specifically tests the ability to decompose complex queries into latent dependency graphs with high indegree, ideal for evaluating $\mathcal{T}_{dag}$ and $\mathcal{T}_{graph}$.

**Baselines**
The proposed TopoReason framework will be compared against the following baseline methodologies:
1.  **Chain-of-Thought (CoT):** The standard linear prompting method (Wei et al., 2022), serving as the baseline for $\mathcal{T}_{chain}$.
2.  **Tree-of-Thoughts (ToT):** A baseline exploring multiple reasoning paths via breadth-first search (Yao et al., 2023), representing $\mathcal{T}_{tree}$.
3.  **Graph of Thoughts (GoT):** The standard graph-based reasoning framework (Besta et al., 2024), representing a static, un-optimized $\mathcal{T}_{graph}$ applied uniformly to all queries.
4.  **RouteGoT:** The state-of-the-art adaptive routing framework that dynamically selects reasoning structures at runtime using heuristic policies. This is the primary competitive baseline for evaluating the efficiency and accuracy of TopoReason's theoretical selection algorithm.

**Metrics**
Performance will be assessed using the following metrics:
1.  **Accuracy ($Acc$):** The primary metric measuring the proportion of correctly answered questions.
    $$ Acc = \frac{\text{Number of Correct Predictions}}{\text{Total Number of Questions}} $$
2.  **Token Consumption ($C_{token}$):** The total number of tokens generated by the LLM during the reasoning process, measuring computational cost.
3.  **Accuracy per Token ($ATR$):** A novel efficiency metric defined as the accuracy achieved per 1,000 tokens consumed, measuring the cost-effectiveness of the reasoning topology.
    $$ ATR = \frac{Acc}{C_{token} / 1000} $$
4.  **Topology Prediction Accuracy ($TPA$):** The accuracy of the TopoReason's dependency extractor in predicting the optimal topology compared to an oracle baseline (the topology that actually achieves the highest accuracy in hindsight).
    $$ TPA = \frac{\text{Number of Correct Topology Predictions}}{\text{Total Number of Questions}} $$

**Ablation Design**
To isolate the contribution of individual components, we propose the following ablation experiments:
1.  **Ablation on Dependency Extraction:** Replace the lightweight LLM-based dependency extractor with a trivial heuristic (e.g., always predict $\mathcal{T}_{chain}$ or randomly select a topology). This measures the value added by the latent dependency graph extraction.
2.  **Ablation on Complexity Bounds:** Relax the theoretical complexity bounds and use a simplified routing heuristic based solely on prompt length or surface-level keyword matching. This tests the necessity of the formal Kolmogorov complexity-based bounds for optimal routing.
3.  **Ablation on Base LLM:** Conduct experiments using different underlying LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama-3-70B) to ensure the theoretical bounds hold across varying model scales and architectures.

## Risk Mitigation

**Risk 1: Mathematical Intractability of Formal Error Bounds**
The derivation of formal error bounds using Kolmogorov complexity may prove mathematically intractable or rely on idealized assumptions that do not hold for stochastic, autoregressive LLMs.
*   **Mitigation Strategy:** Focus the initial theoretical derivation on simplified, bounded models of LLM error propagation (e.g., assuming a fixed base error rate $p_0$ per atomic step) before attempting to integrate full Kolmogorov complexity.
*   **Fallback Plan:** If rigorous proofs are infeasible, pivot to providing empirically grounded "semi-theoretical" bounds. These would be formulas parameterized by constants fitted on a held-out validation set, maintaining the structural intuition of the framework while acknowledging the practical limitations of formalizing stochastic LLM behavior.

**Risk 2: Inaccurate Latent Dependency Graph Extraction**
The lightweight auxiliary LLM may frequently fail to accurately extract the true latent dependency graph ($\hat{\mathcal{D}}$) from the prompt, causing the topology predictor to fail before the theoretical framework can be applied.
*   **Mitigation Strategy:** Employ a robust, structured extraction protocol using few-shot prompting with diverse examples. Validate the extraction accuracy on a small, manually annotated gold-standard dataset before full deployment.
*   **Fallback Plan:** If LLM-based extraction proves unreliable, replace it with a deterministic, syntax-based heuristic parser that estimates dependency depth and breadth using linguistic features (e.g., counting subordinate clauses or logical connectives like "therefore" and "however").

**Risk 3: Misalignment Between Theoretical Predictions and Empirical Results**
The theoretical bounds predicted by the complexity analysis may not align with the messy empirical results generated by stochastic LLM inference, making it difficult to validate the framework's practical utility.
*   **Mitigation Strategy:** Evaluate the framework's alignment not just on exact numerical match, but on the *rank correlation* between predicted and observed efficiency (e.g., does the theory correctly predict which topology will be most efficient, even if the exact token count is off?).
*   **Fallback Plan:** If strict alignment fails, reframe the evaluation to focus on the comparative advantage of the TopoReason selection algorithm over baseline adaptive methods (like RouteGoT), emphasizing practical utility over strict theoretical validation.

## 2. Axiom: A Standardized Benchmark Suite for Cost-Efficiency vs. Reasoning Fidelity Trade-offs

## Title

**Title**

Axiom: Standardized Benchmarks for Cost-Efficiency versus Reasoning Fidelity Trade-offs in Large Language Models

The rapid proliferation of advanced structural reasoning frameworks for Large Language Models (LLMs)—most notably Chain-of-Thought (CoT), Tree-of-Thoughts (ToT), and Graph-of-Thoughts (GoT)—has yielded unprecedented improvements in logical, algorithmic, and commonsense task accuracy. However, these sophisticated paradigms fundamentally rely on expansive search trajectories and iterative refinement, incurring drastically elevated inference costs through exponential token consumption, heightened compute overhead, and severe latency penalties. Despite the critical operational implications of these resource demands, the field currently lacks a unified, empirical framework to quantitatively evaluate the inherent compromises between reasoning fidelity and computational expenditure. To bridge this pivotal gap, we introduce "Axiom," the first comprehensive, standardized benchmark suite explicitly engineered to evaluate structural LLM reasoning under strict resource constraints. By systematically evaluating varied reasoning architectures against curated, structurally annotated datasets utilizing a novel "Reasoning Yield Quotient" (RYQ) metric, Axiom establishes an essential empirical baseline. This work provides the necessary data-driven framework to guide industry practitioners in optimizing the complex trade-offs between accuracy, latency, and operational budget, thereby accelerating the viable deployment of advanced reasoning frameworks in real-world production environments.

## Abstract

Advanced structural reasoning frameworks for Large Language Models (LLMs), such as Tree-of-Thoughts (ToT) and Graph-of-Thoughts (GoT), significantly enhance problem-solving accuracy by exploring multiple inference pathways. However, these paradigms introduce severe computational overhead, drastically increasing inference latency, token consumption, and financial cost. The field currently lacks a standardized empirical benchmark to evaluate the complex trade-offs between reasoning fidelity and resource constraints, severely hindering the deployment of these frameworks in production environments. This proposal introduces Axiom, a comprehensive benchmark suite designed to systematically evaluate CoT, ToT, and GoT variations under strict resource budgets. Axiom provides a curated dataset taxonomy annotated for structural complexity, a unified inference API wrapper enforcing hard resource constraints, and a novel evaluation metric, the Reasoning Yield Quotient (RYQ). The RYQ quantifies the marginal accuracy improvement per unit of computational cost relative to a standardized baseline. By executing Axiom across diverse LLM scales and plotting accuracy versus token cost curves, this work will establish the first empirical baseline for structural LLM reasoning under constraint. The resulting guidelines will empower practitioners to select optimal reasoning paradigms based on specific latency, budget, and accuracy requirements, ultimately accelerating the transition of structural reasoning research into real-world applications.

## Introduction

The advent of large language models (LLMs) has ushered in a new era of complex problem-solving capabilities, largely catalyzed by the introduction of advanced prompt engineering and reasoning frameworks. While standard Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting established the foundational baseline for step-by-step logical deduction, the pursuit of higher reasoning fidelity has driven the field toward increasingly sophisticated structural paradigms. Frameworks such as Tree-of-Thoughts (ToT) and Graph-of-Thoughts (GoT) have demonstrated remarkable improvements in accuracy across algorithmic, logical, and mathematical domains by enabling models to explore, backtrack, and aggregate multiple reasoning paths. However, this enhanced cognitive capability comes at a steep systemic cost. By traversing expansive state spaces and maintaining intricate contextual graphs, these advanced frameworks drastically inflate inference overhead—manifesting as exponential increases in token consumption, computational load, and end-user latency. As the industry shifts its focus from mere capability to sustainable deployment, the tension between reasoning fidelity and resource constraints has emerged as a critical bottleneck.

Despite the rapid proliferation of these structural reasoning frameworks, the field currently lacks a standardized, empirical paradigm for evaluating the inherent trade-offs between accuracy and computational overhead. Existing benchmarks overwhelmingly evaluate reasoning frameworks in resource-unconstrained settings, optimizing solely for maximum accuracy while ignoring the practical limitations of production environments. This blind spot creates a significant barrier to entry for industry practitioners who must operate within strict latency ceilings and finite compute budgets. Without a unified benchmark to assess how different reasoning topologies perform under specific resource constraints, it remains exceedingly difficult to justify the deployment of computationally heavy methods like GoT over leaner approaches like CoT. Furthermore, current evaluation metrics fail to capture the nuanced economic realities of LLM deployment; there is no standardized metric that quantifies the marginal utility of generated tokens in terms of reasoning accuracy. Consequently, the transition of advanced reasoning research into real-world, scalable applications remains largely heuristic and poorly informed.

To bridge this critical gap, we propose "Axiom," a comprehensive and standardized benchmark suite explicitly designed to evaluate the cost-efficiency versus reasoning fidelity trade-offs in modern LLM reasoning frameworks. Axiom introduces a three-pronged evaluation methodology. First, it leverages a curated dataset taxonomy spanning algorithmic, logical, and commonsense reasoning domains, wherein existing datasets are specifically annotated for "structural complexity"—an estimation of the minimum graph depth and width required to reach a correct solution. Second, Axiom utilizes a unified inference API wrapper that enforces strict, customizable resource budgets, allowing researchers to test models under rigid constraints such as maximum token limits or strict latency ceilings. Finally, the suite conducts a rigorous, head-to-head evaluation of CoT, ToT, and GoT variations (including dynamic routing approaches like RouteGoT) across these constrained environments. Central to our methodology is the introduction of a novel evaluation metric, the "Reasoning Yield Quotient" (RYQ). The RYQ quantifies the efficiency of a reasoning framework by measuring the marginal accuracy improvement per thousand tokens generated, evaluated relative to a standard zero-shot baseline. This provides a mathematically rigorous lens through which to view the economic viability of complex prompting strategies.

The contributions of this research are foundational and highly practical for both the academic community and industry practitioners. Axiom will establish the first comprehensive, standardized empirical baseline for evaluating structural LLM reasoning under strict resource constraints. By exposing the actual cost of cognitive expansion in LLMs, this benchmark will shift the paradigm of reasoning evaluation from unconstrained accuracy maximization to optimized resource allocation. Furthermore, this work will yield concrete, data-driven guidelines to assist practitioners in selecting the optimal reasoning paradigm based on their specific latency, budget, and accuracy requirements. Ultimately, Axiom will demystify the operational costs of advanced reasoning frameworks, accelerating the translation of complex structural reasoning research into efficient, scalable, and economically viable real-world applications.

## Related Work

The proposed Axiom benchmark intersects with three distinct research directions: structural reasoning frameworks, cost-adaptive inference, and LLM benchmarking. Understanding the trajectory of these fields highlights the critical necessity for a unified evaluation suite.

**Structural Reasoning Frameworks.** The evolution of LLM reasoning began with Chain-of-Thought (CoT), which elicits step-by-step logical progression. Recognizing the limitations of linear paths, Yao et al. introduced Tree-of-Thoughts (ToT), which frames reasoning as a search problem over a tree structure, allowing the model to explore multiple reasoning paths and backtrack. Taking this complexity further, Besta et al. (2024) proposed Graph-of-Thoughts (GoT), which models the reasoning process as a directed acyclic graph. GoT allows for the merging of disparate reasoning paths and the iterative refinement of thoughts. While these frameworks demonstrate profound accuracy improvements on datasets like GSM8K and MATH, their primary limitation is a near-exclusive focus on maximizing accuracy while treating computational cost as a secondary, often unreported, externality. Axiom directly addresses this by evaluating these topologies strictly through the lens of cost constraints.

**Cost-Adaptive Inference.** Recently, the community has recognized the computational burden of complex reasoning. Works such as RouteGoT have begun exploring node-adaptive routing to manage the computational costs of GoT structures, attempting to dynamically allocate compute only to complex nodes. Similarly, research into adaptive early-exit mechanisms and dynamic token routing aims to reduce inference latency. However, these approaches are inherently point-solutions; they propose specific algorithms to reduce cost but lack a broad, standardized empirical foundation to validate their efficacy against one another. The limitation is the absence of a common evaluation protocol. Axiom provides the missing empirical harness, offering a standardized environment where cost-adaptive methods can be fairly compared under identical, strict resource budgets.

**LLM Benchmarking and Evaluation.** The NLP community relies heavily on standardized benchmarks to track progress. General-purpose benchmarks like MMLU and HELM evaluate vast knowledge and basic reasoning but do not account for the specific token-by-token cost of dynamic inference strategies. Mathematical benchmarks, such as those utilized by (Friedman, 1991) and explored in the mathematical capabilities of ChatGPT, measure raw problem-solving capability but fail to capture the cost-fidelity trade-off. While Dwivedi et al. (2023) discuss the broad implications of generative AI, and Yang et al. (2024) provide practical guides for LLM downstream tasks, there is no established benchmark tailored to the cost-benefit analysis of structural reasoning topologies. Axiom fills this gap by introducing the Reasoning Yield Quotient (RYQ) and budget-constrained evaluation, establishing a new paradigm for benchmarking reasoning efficiency.

## Proposed Method

The Axiom benchmark suite is founded on a rigorous mathematical formulation designed to quantify the trade-offs between reasoning fidelity and resource consumption. The core methodology is divided into three interconnected components: a formal problem definition, the budget-constrained inference wrapper, and the Reasoning Yield Quotient (RYQ) metric.

**Formal Problem Definition.**
Let $D = \{(q_i, a_i, c_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ represent our curated benchmark dataset, where $q_i$ is a natural language question, $a_i$ is the ground-truth answer, and $c_i \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ is the annotated structural complexity score (representing the expected minimum graph depth and width required to solve the problem). 

Let $M$ denote a Large Language Model and $T \in \{\text{CoT}, \text{ToT}, \text{GoT}\}$ denote a structural reasoning topology. The execution of topology $T$ on question $q_i$ using model $M$ yields a predicted answer $\hat{a}_i$ and consumes a specific amount of resources. We define the resource consumption vector as $R(T, M, q_i) = [\tau_{tokens}, \tau_{latency}, \tau_{compute}]$, representing total tokens generated, wall-clock latency, and computational FLOPs respectively. 

The goal of Axiom is to characterize the function $f: \mathcal{T} \times \mathcal{M} \times \mathcal{D} \rightarrow \mathcal{A} \times \mathcal{R}$, mapping topology choices and resource budgets to final accuracies. We define a strict resource budget $B = [\beta_{tokens}, \beta_{latency}]$. The objective of any reasoning framework under evaluation is to maximize the expected accuracy subject to the resource constraint:

$$ \max_{T} \mathbb{E}[I(\hat{a}_i = a_i)] \quad \text{subject to} \quad R(T, M, q_i) \leq B $$

where $I(\cdot)$ is the indicator function.

**Budget-Constrained Inference Wrapper.**
To ensure strict adherence to the resource budget $B$, Axiom utilizes a unified inference API wrapper. Unlike standard evaluations that allow a framework to run to completion and measure cost post-hoc, the Axiom wrapper enforces a hard constraint. 

During the execution of a topology $T$ (such as a Graph-of-Thought expansion), the wrapper continuously monitors the accumulated token count $\tau_{tokens}$ and the elapsed time $\tau_{latency}$. If a framework attempts to generate a new thought node $v_k$ and the predicted resource consumption exceeds the budget ($\hat{R}(v_k) + \tau_{current} > B$), the wrapper immediately triggers an interrupt. The reasoning graph is forcibly truncated, and an aggregator prompt is injected into the LLM, forcing it to synthesize a final answer $\hat{a}_i$ strictly from the currently explored sub-graph. This simulates real-world SLA enforcement and tests the graceful degradation capabilities of different topologies.

**Reasoning Yield Quotient (RYQ).**
To compare the cost-efficiency of different reasoning paradigms, we introduce the Reasoning Yield Quotient (RYQ). Traditional accuracy metrics fail to account for the massive cost disparities between CoT and GoT. Let $Acc(T, B)$ denote the accuracy of topology $T$ under budget $B$, and let $Cost(T, B)$ denote the average token consumption (in thousands of tokens, $K$) for successful evaluations.

The RYQ measures the marginal accuracy improvement per unit of token cost relative to a standardized zero-shot baseline:

$$ RYQ(T, B) = \frac{Acc(T, B) - Acc(\text{Zero-Shot}, \infty)}{Cost(T, B)} \times 100 $$

Here, $Acc(\text{Zero-Shot}, \infty)$ represents the baseline accuracy of the model using a simple prompt with no structural reasoning and infinite budget. The multiplication by 100 scales the metric for readability. A higher RYQ indicates a more cost-efficient reasoning strategy.

## Evaluation Plan

**Datasets**
The Axiom benchmark will utilize a curated taxonomy of three widely-adopted reasoning datasets, representing varying levels of structural complexity:
1. **GSM8K** (grade school math): 8,792 problems. Source: https://huggingface.co/datasets/gsm8k. Annotated with low structural complexity ($c_i \in [1, 3]$), representing problems where linear CoT is often sufficient.
2. **MATH** (competition mathematics): 7,500 problems. Source: https://huggingface.co/datasets/math_dataset. Annotated with high structural complexity ($c_i \in [4, 7]$), representing problems requiring significant backtracking and multi-path exploration.
3. **LogiQA** (logical reasoning): 8,678 problems. Source: https://huggingface.co/datasets/lucasmccabe/logiqa. Annotated with medium structural complexity ($c_i \in [2, 5]$), testing deductive logic over natural language.

**Baselines**
We will evaluate the following baseline reasoning paradigms within the Axiom wrapper:
1. **Zero-Shot Prompting:** Direct question-answering without intermediate steps.
2. **Chain-of-Thought (CoT):** Standard linear reasoning prompting as established by Wei et al.
3. **Tree-of-Thoughts (ToT):** Breadth-first search over a tree of reasoning paths (Yao et al.).
4. **Graph-of-Thoughts (GoT):** Full graph expansion allowing thought aggregation and refinement (Besta et al., 2024).
5. **RouteGoT:** A recent cost-adaptive variation of GoT that uses node-adaptive routing to minimize unnecessary compute.

**Metrics**
Performance will be primarily evaluated using the following metrics:
1. **Accuracy ($Acc$):** The percentage of questions where the exact match of the final answer $\hat{a}_i$ matches the ground truth $a_i$.
2. **Token Consumption ($Cost$):** The total number of tokens generated per query, measured in thousands ($K$).
3. **Reasoning Yield Quotient ($RYQ$):** As formally defined in the Proposed Method: $RYQ(T, B) = \frac{Acc(T, B) - Acc(\text{Zero-Shot}, \infty)}{Cost(T, B)} \times 100$.

**Ablation Design**
To understand the mechanics of cost-fidelity trade-offs, we will conduct the following ablation experiments:
1. **Budget Gradient Ablation:** We will evaluate all topologies across a strict gradient of token budgets (e.g., $B = [100, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000]$ tokens). This will map the exact inflection points where complex topologies (GoT) begin to outperform simple topologies (CoT) as budgets expand.
2. **Complexity Stratification Ablation:** We will isolate model performance on data subsets stratified by our structural complexity annotation ($c_i$). This ablation will validate the hypothesis that high-complexity problems require graph topologies to achieve optimal RYQ, while low-complexity problems suffer a negative RYQ when forced into graph structures.

## Risk Mitigation

**Risk 1: API Cost and Rate Limits.** Running extensive ablations on proprietary models (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) under complex GoT frameworks could become prohibitively expensive and slow. 
*Mitigation:* We will implement aggressive caching mechanisms for intermediate thought nodes to avoid redundant API calls. Furthermore, we will utilize asynchronous request routing and distribute API calls across multiple account endpoints to bypass rate limits. 
*Fallback Plan:* If proprietary API costs exceed the allocated budget, we will pivot to utilizing quantized open-weights models (e.g., Llama-3 70B AWQ, Mixtral 8x22B) hosted on local infrastructure to approximate the frontier model behavior without incurring per-token costs.

**Risk 2: Metric Adoption Resistance.** The proposed Reasoning Yield Quotient (RYQ) might face community resistance if the baseline zero-shot prompt is perceived as inconsistent across diverse task domains.
*Mitigation:* We will rigorously standardize the zero-shot baseline prompt across all datasets, ensuring it represents a best-effort zero-shot attempt. We will also transparently report raw accuracy and token counts alongside RYQ.
*Fallback Plan:* If RYQ proves controversial, we will pivot to reporting standard Pareto optimal frontiers (accuracy vs. cost curves) as the primary evaluative framework, offering RYQ strictly as a supplementary heuristic.

**Risk 3: Engineering Overhead.** Unifying disparate reasoning frameworks into a single wrapper with flawless budget enforcement (e.g., hard stopping mid-generation) requires significant engineering effort.
*Mitigation:* We will allocate the first three weeks strictly to wrapper engineering and utilize robust, asynchronous Python frameworks (like `asyncio`) to manage interrupts cleanly. 
*Fallback Plan:* If hard-stop interrupts prove technically infeasible at the exact token level due to API streaming limitations, we will fall back to a "soft budget" approach, allowing the current thought step to finish before triggering the forced aggregation, while still strictly enforcing the overall query latency limit.
