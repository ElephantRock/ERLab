## Abstract

Autonomous Large Language Model (LLM) agents are increasingly deployed in complex, high-stakes domains such as cybersecurity and healthcare, yet their decision-making trajectories remain largely opaque. There is a critical lack of methodologies to validate whether these agents utilize genuine causal reasoning or merely exploit spurious correlations in their environment, posing a significant risk for safety-critical applications. To address this, CausalTrajectory, a neuro-symbolic framework that integrates Structural Causal Models (SCMs) directly into an agent's planning loop, is proposed. The framework introduces a novel Causal Replay mechanism that applies interventional validation via do-calculus to an agent's past decision trajectories. By systematically perturbing tool inputs and state observations, the method generates formal, human-readable causal attribution graphs explaining why an agent executed a specific action. This approach bridges the gap between LLM explainability and formal causal inference. The expected results include a robust, mathematically grounded metric for Causal Grounding in LLM planning, and a practical auditing tool that significantly improves the transparency and reliability of autonomous AI systems in dynamic environments.

## Introduction

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has catalyzed a paradigm shift in artificial intelligence, moving from static, single-turn text generators to dynamic, autonomous agents capable of complex reasoning and environmental interaction. By integrating foundation models with tool-use capabilities and memory architectures, these agents can now perform multi-step tasks in domains previously thought to require human intuition and expertise. High-stakes applications, such as automated vulnerability discovery in cybersecurity (Boiko et al., 2023) and autonomous chemical synthesis, highlight the transformative potential of these systems. In these environments, agents must navigate complex state spaces, execute precise tool-oriented actions, and adapt to dynamic feedback. However, this unprecedented autonomy introduces severe safety vulnerabilities. As these systems are granted the authority to execute impactful real-world actions, the opacity of their internal decision-making processes becomes a critical liability. Without a clear understanding of *why* an agent chose a specific trajectory, deploying these systems in safety-critical infrastructure is akin to granting unchecked authority to a black box.

Despite the remarkable performance of LLM agents, current approaches to understanding their behavior remain fundamentally inadequate. Standard prompting techniques, such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT), provide a narrative of the model's reasoning but are highly susceptible to hallucination and post-hoc rationalization. An agent might articulate a logically sound step-by-step plan while actually relying on superficial spurious correlations present in its training data or environmental observations. Furthermore, existing Explainable AI (XAI) methods for deep learning, such as gradient-based saliency maps, are ill-suited for the discrete, multi-modal, and tool-augmented action spaces characteristic of modern LLM agents (Ras et al., 2022). Recent surveys on LLM agents (Lei et al., 2025) emphasize that ensuring reliability and formal verification for these autonomous architectures remains a largely unresolved challenge. The field critically lacks mechanisms to distinguish between an agent that arrives at the correct answer through robust causal deduction and one that succeeds by exploiting coincidental environmental shortcuts.

To bridge this gap between agent capabilities and formal explainability, CausalTrajectory, a neuro-symbolic framework designed for the interventional validation and explainability of autonomous LLM agents, is proposed. The core innovation of this framework is the formalization of an agent's decision trajectory as a Structural Causal Model (SCM). By introducing a Causal Replay mechanism, the method applies Pearl’s do-calculus to systematically perturb past tool inputs and state observations. Unlike static prompting methods, CausalTrajectory actively probes the agent's planning loop to isolate the true causal drivers of its actions, generating formal, human-readable causal attribution graphs. This approach uniquely synthesizes the robust mathematical foundations of causal inference with the practical needs of LLM agent auditing, moving beyond purely observational explainability to rigorous interventional validation.

The expected contributions of this research are threefold. First, a novel neuro-symbolic framework that integrates SCMs directly into the agent planning loop for real-time causal validation is formalized. Second, a rigorous evaluation metric, Causal Grounding Score ($CGS$), is introduced to quantify the degree to which an agent relies on genuine causal reasoning versus spurious correlations. Finally, a comprehensive empirical evaluation using the AgentInstruct dataset and cybersecurity Capture The Flag (CTF) environments is provided, demonstrating the framework's ability to accurately audit agent behavior and improve robustness against adversarial environmental perturbations.

## Related Work

## Related Work

**Explainability and Auditing in Autonomous LLM Agents.** As LLM-based agents are increasingly deployed in complex, interactive environments (Yao et al., 2023; Significant Gravitas, 2023), understanding their decision-making processes has become a critical bottleneck. Current explainability methods for autonomous agents predominantly rely on post-hoc natural language rationalization, where the model is prompted to articulate its reasoning steps (Wei et al., 2022). While Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting and its derivatives offer a degree of transparency, recent studies demonstrate that these verbal rationales frequently misrepresent the true computational substrates driving the model's outputs, a phenomenon characterized as "clever Hans" effects or mechanistic unfaithfulness (Turpin et al., 2023). Tool-based auditing frameworks, such as those evaluating agents in cybersecurity Capture The Flag (CTF) environments or software engineering benchmarks, effectively measure end-task performance but fail to provide formal, structural explanations for *why* a specific action was selected (Shinn et al., 2023). CausalTrajectory addresses this gap by moving beyond correlational post-hoc explanations, generating formal, human-readable causal attribution graphs that mathematically isolate the true determinants of an agent's action. 

**Causal Reasoning and Large Language Models.** The intersection of formal causal inference and large language models is an rapidly evolving domain. Initial research has primarily focused on evaluating LLMs' inherent capabilities to parse causal directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) or answer counterfactual queries in zero-shot settings (Kıcıman et al., 2023). More recent approaches have attempted to embed causal structures into prompts to improve logical consistency (Jin et al., 2023). However, these applications largely treat causal reasoning as a static text-inference task rather than an interactive, interventional process. Conversely, algorithmic recourse and interventional validation techniques rooted in Pearl’s do-calculus (Pearl, 2009) have seen limited application to the sequential decision-making trajectories of autonomous agents. By introducing the 'Causal Replay' mechanism, our work operationalizes interventional validation (do-calculus) directly within the agent's planning loop. To our knowledge, this is the first framework that systematically perturbs tool inputs and state observations post-hoc to formally validate whether an agent is utilizing genuine causal reasoning or exploiting spurious environmental correlations.

**Neuro-Symbolic Planning and Formal Validation.** The integration of symbolic logic into neural architectures—neuro-symbolic AI—has long been proposed as a mechanism to imbue deep learning systems with formal verifiability and safety guarantees (Garcez et al., 2019). In the context of LLM planning, contemporary neuro-symbolic frameworks typically use symbolic modules (such as PDDL planners or Python executors) to constrain LLM outputs or enforce syntax (Liu et al., 2023). While these methods reduce hallucinations and syntax errors, they do not evaluate the causal grounding of the LLM's internal state representations. CausalTrajectory bridges the gap between LLM explainability and formal causal inference by integrating Structural Causal Models (SCMs) directly into the neuro-symbolic planning loop. By leveraging highly accessible, open-source environments (e.g., AgentInstruct and standard CTF environments) for interventional replay, our framework provides a highly feasible, formally rigorous, and practically deployable tool for auditing AI agent decisions in safety-critical applications.

## Proposed Method

The CausalTrajectory framework is designed to audit, explain, and validate the sequential decision-making processes of autonomous LLM agents. The methodology bridges the gap between the latent, unstructured reasoning of neural networks and the rigorous, formal domain of structural causal models (SCMs). The system operates in three primary phases: (1) Trajectory Formalization, where the agent's interaction history is mapped to a structural causal model; (2) Causal Replay, an interventional mechanism applying do-calculus to test causal dependencies; and (3) Causal Attribution, the generation of human-readable graphs and a quantitative Causal Grounding Score.

**Formal Problem Definition**
Consider an autonomous LLM agent operating within an environment $\mathcal{E}$ over a discrete time horizon $t \in \{1, 2, ..., T\}$. At each step $t$, the agent receives a state observation $O_t$ and maintains an internal reasoning trace $R_t$. Based on these, the agent selects an action $A_t$ (which may include invoking a specific tool with a set of parameters). The complete decision trajectory is defined as the sequence $\tau = \{(O_1, R_1, A_1), (O_2, R_2, A_2), ..., (O_T, R_T, A_T)\}$. 

The central problem is to determine whether the action $A_t$ at a specific step $t$ is causally dependent on a critical observation $O_k$ (where $k \leq t$), or if $A_t$ is the result of a spurious correlation or hallucinated reasoning independent of the true environmental state. We formulate the trajectory $\tau$ as a Structural Causal Model (SCM) $\mathcal{M} = \langle \mathbf{U}, \mathbf{V}, \mathcal{F}, P(\mathbf{U}) \rangle$. Here, $\mathbf{V}$ represents the set of endogenous variables corresponding to the elements of the trajectory, specifically $\mathbf{V} = \{O_1, R_1, A_1, ..., O_T, R_T, A_T\}$. $\mathbf{U}$ represents the set of exogenous, unobserved variables (such as the LLM's latent prompt context or inherent stochasticity). $\mathcal{F}$ is the set of structural functions determining the values of the endogenous variables, and $P(\mathbf{U})$ is the distribution over exogenous factors.

**Trajectory Formalization and SCM Construction**
To build the SCM, we define the structural equations $\mathcal{F}$ that dictate the relationships between variables in the agent's planning loop. The action $A_t$ is modeled as a function of the current observation $O_t$ and the preceding reasoning trace $R_{t-1}$:
$$A_t := f_A(O_t, R_{t-1}, U_A)$$
The reasoning trace $R_t$ is similarly modeled as a function of the observation $O_t$ and the previous action $A_t$:
$$R_t := f_R(O_t, A_t, U_R)$$
In the CausalTrajectory framework, the functions $f_A$ and $f_R$ are approximated by the LLM itself. To extract the causal graph $\mathcal{G}$ associated with $\mathcal{M}$, we analyze the attention mechanisms and prompt dependencies of the agent, establishing a directed acyclic graph (DAG) where nodes are elements of $\mathbf{V}$ and edges represent direct causal influences.

**Causal Replay and Interventional Validation**
The core algorithmic innovation is the Causal Replay mechanism. Standard observational conditioning is insufficient to distinguish causation from correlation due to the presence of confounders (e.g., a global system prompt that influences both the observation parsing and the action selection). To isolate the causal effect of an observation $O_k$ on an action $A_t$, we employ the $do$-operator.

We define an interventional trajectory $\tau_{do(O_k = o'_k)}$ where the original observation $O_k$ is surgically replaced with a counterfactual or perturbed observation $o'_k$, while keeping all other exogenous variables $\mathbf{U}$ fixed. The interventional distribution of the action $A_t$ is given by:
$$P(A_t \mid do(O_k = o'_k)) = \sum_{u} P(A_t \mid O_k = o'_k, Pa(A_t) \setminus O_k, u) P(u)$$
where $Pa(A_t)$ denotes the parents of $A_t$ in the causal graph $\mathcal{G}$. 

Algorithmically, the Causal Replay mechanism executes the following steps:
1. Identify a critical action $A_t$ in the historical trajectory $\tau$ that requires explanation.
2. Select a target observation variable $O_k$ (where $k \leq t$) hypothesized to have driven $A_t$.
3. Generate a perturbed state $o'_k$ using a perturbation function $\phi(O_k)$, which may mask critical information, inject adversarial noise, or swap entities.
4. Re-run the LLM agent's planning loop from step $k$ to $t$, forcing the observation $O_k = o'_k$ but allowing the agent to freely generate subsequent reasoning and actions.
5. Compare the resulting interventional action $A_t^{int}$ with the original factual action $A_t^{fac}$.

**Causal Attribution and Grounding Metric**
To quantify the degree to which an agent relies on genuine causal reasoning, we introduce the Causal Grounding Score ($CGS$). The $CGS$ measures the sensitivity of an action $A_t$ to systematic interventions on its parent observations. For a specific action $A_t$, the Causal Grounding Score is defined as the expected distance between the factual action distribution and the interventional action distribution across a set of targeted interventions $\mathbb{I}$:
$$CGS(A_t) = \mathbb{E}_{O_k \in Pa(A_t)} \left[ 1 - \text{Sim}\left( A_t^{fac}, A_t^{do(O_k=\phi(O_k))} \right) \right]$$
where $\text{Sim}(\cdot, \cdot)$ is a semantic similarity metric (e.g., BERTScore or exact match for tool execution APIs). A high $CGS$ indicates high causal grounding, meaning the agent's actions are tightly coupled with the environmental observations. A low $CGS$ indicates that the agent is relying on spurious correlations or prior biases, ignoring the true state of the environment.

This allows developers to automatically generate human-readable causal attribution graphs explaining specific agent actions, drastically improving transparency and trust.

## Evaluation Plan

**Datasets**
The evaluation will utilize two primary data sources representing diverse domains of autonomous agent interaction:
1. **AgentInstruct Dataset:** A comprehensive dataset of LLM agent interactions designed for instruction tuning and complex task completion. It contains over 1,800 diverse tasks across various domains requiring tool use and multi-step reasoning. Source: Available via Microsoft Research and standard open-source repositories.
2. **Cybersecurity Capture The Flag (CTF) Environments:** We will utilize Intercode-CTF and picoCTF, which provide interactive, simulated cybersecurity environments. These environments require agents to execute terminal commands, analyze code, and exploit vulnerabilities, offering a high-stakes testbed where causal reasoning is essential to avoid catastrophic failure. 

**Baselines**
The proposed CausalTrajectory framework will be evaluated against the following baseline agent architectures:
1. **ReAct (Yao et al., 2023):** The standard Reason+Act paradigm that interleaves thought traces with action execution. It serves as the foundational agent architecture without explicit causal verification.
2. **Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023):** An extension of ReAct that incorporates linguistic reinforcement learning, allowing the agent to reflect on past failures. This baseline helps isolate the effect of self-correction versus formal causal validation.
3. **LATS (Zhou et al., 2023):** Language Agent Tree Search, which uses Monte Carlo Tree Search for planning. This provides a strong baseline for agents with advanced lookahead planning but lacking structural causal models.

**Metrics**
1. **Task Success Rate ($TSR$):** The primary functional metric, defined as the ratio of successfully completed tasks to the total attempted tasks: $TSR = \frac{N_{success}}{N_{total}}$.
2. **Causal Grounding Score ($CGS$):** The proposed metric, measuring the sensitivity of the agent's actions to interventional perturbations, as defined in the methodology section.
3. **Attribution Accuracy ($AA$):** Measures the accuracy of the generated causal attribution graphs compared to human expert annotations. It is calculated as the F1-score of the edges in the predicted causal graph compared to the ground-truth human graph: $AA = \frac{2 \cdot |E_{pred} \cap E_{true}|}{|E_{pred}| + |E_{true}|}$.
4. **Robustness to Spurious Perturbations ($RSP$):** Measures the degradation in task performance when the environment is intentionally injected with distracting, spurious context. $RSP = \frac{TSR_{perturbed}}{TSR_{clean}}$.

**Ablation Design**
To understand the contribution of individual components in CausalTrajectory, we propose the following ablation experiments:
1. **Observational vs. Interventional SCM:** We will compare the full CausalTrajectory framework (using do-calculus interventions) against an ablated version that constructs the causal graph using purely observational correlations (standard attention weights or prompting). This tests whether interventional validation is strictly necessary for accurate causal attribution.
2. **Perturbation Strategy Ablation:** We will evaluate the impact of the perturbation function $\phi$ used in the Causal Replay mechanism. We will compare random token masking, entity swapping, and adversarial negation to determine the optimal strategy for eliciting causal dependencies in the agent's reasoning trace.

## Risk Mitigation

**Risk 1: Intractability of Formalizing LLM Reasoning into SCMs.** 
Mapping the highly non-linear, latent reasoning process of an LLM into a strict Structural Causal Model may prove intractable or require severe oversimplifications, undermining the framework's mathematical rigor. 
*Mitigation Strategy:* We will employ a "semi-symbolic" abstraction layer. Rather than attempting to model the continuous, high-dimensional internal activations of the LLM, the SCM will be constructed strictly at the semantic level—modeling the causal relationships between discrete, observable variables (inputs, text outputs, and tool API calls). This bypasses the need to interpret latent neural states.
*Fallback Plan:* If dynamic SCM construction remains too brittle, we will utilize static, domain-specific causal graph templates (e.g., pre-defined graphs for terminal command execution) and evaluate the agent's adherence to these known logical structures.

**Risk 2: Resource Intensity of Expert Annotations.**
Generating ground-truth, expert-annotated causal attribution graphs for the evaluation phase will be highly resource-intensive, expensive, and difficult to scale, potentially limiting the size of the evaluation dataset.
*Mitigation Strategy:* We will employ a targeted sampling strategy, utilizing active learning principles to select only the most ambiguous or critical decision trajectories for expert annotation. Furthermore, we will use a consensus protocol (e.g., Krippendorff's alpha) to ensure inter-rater reliability among a small, highly qualified group of cybersecurity experts.
*Fallback Plan:* If acquiring sufficient expert annotations proves infeasible, we will pivot to synthetic ground-truth generation. We will construct deterministic rule-based agents (acting as "expert systems") within the CTF environments, using their logically perfect execution traces as proxies for ground-truth causal graphs.

**Risk 3: Computational Overhead of Causal Replay.**
The computational overhead and latency of running Causal Replay and do-calculus interventions during the planning loop might make the agent too slow for real-time or interactive environments like CTFs.
*Mitigation Strategy:* CausalTrajectory is designed primarily as a *post-hoc auditing tool* rather than a strict real-time planning mechanism. The interventional validation will be executed asynchronously on recorded trajectories after a task is completed, ensuring no degradation of the agent's interactive performance.
*Fallback Plan:* If real-time integration is strictly required for a specific application, we will restrict the Causal Replay to a lightweight, sampled subset of possible interventions (e.g., intervening only on the most recent observation $O_t$ rather than the entire history), significantly reducing computational latency at the cost of exhaustive completeness.

CogniSwitch: Translating Human Cognitive Control Frameworks for Multi-Task LLM Agents

The proposed research introduces "CogniSwitch," a pioneering architectural and algorithmic framework designed to bridge the profound disconnect between cognitive science’s understanding of human executive control and the current structural limitations of autonomous multi-tasking artificial intelligence agents. While modern Large Language Models (LLMs) exhibit remarkable proficiency in isolated tasks, they frequently succumb to catastrophic interference and inefficient context switching when confronted with complex, interleaved multi-task scenarios. To address this critical bottleneck, CogniSwitch translates established psychological theories of human goal maintenance and executive control into a robust computational model. By operationalizing an innovative "Episodic Buffer of Thoughts" to sustain task-specific rule schemas, alongside a dynamic "Control Signal" mechanism engineered to actively re-weight attention heads and suppress inactive task interference, this research establishes a biologically-inspired paradigm. Ultimately, this work not only aims to significantly mitigate multi-task degradation in artificial agents but also to establish a foundational, interdisciplinary framework for evaluating cognitive flexibility and advancing the landscape of autonomous agent architectures.

## Abstract

The deployment of autonomous Large Language Model (LLM) agents in complex, real-world environments is fundamentally constrained by their susceptibility to catastrophic interference and inefficient context switching during multi-task interleaving. While cognitive science has long established robust frameworks for understanding human executive control and goal maintenance, current AI architectures lack computationally actionable translations of these biological mechanisms. This proposal introduces CogniSwitch, a novel cognitive-control-inspired architecture designed to mitigate multi-task interference in LLM agents. The proposed approach operationalizes human psychological theories of task switching through an "Episodic Buffer of Thoughts" that maintains task-specific rule schemas, coupled with an algorithmic "Control Signal" mechanism that dynamically re-weights the LLM's attention heads to suppress interference from inactive task contexts. The expected result is a significant reduction in multi-task interference errors and improved cognitive flexibility in artificial agents, validated through a novel multi-task embodied text environment. The anticipated contributions include a biologically-inspired computational model for AI task switching, empirical demonstrations of reduced error rates, and the introduction of a standardized evaluation framework for measuring cognitive flexibility in autonomous systems.

## Introduction

The rapid advancement of Large Language Model (LLM) agents represents a paradigm shift in artificial intelligence, combining sophisticated reasoning capabilities with the ability to perceive environments and execute complex action sequences (Yiming et al., 2025). As these agents are increasingly deployed in dynamic, real-world scenarios—ranging from autonomous scientific research (Boiko et al., 2023) to personalized career guidance (Asai et al., 2023)—they are expected to manage multiple, interleaved tasks simultaneously. A human chef, for instance, seamlessly alternates between chopping vegetables, monitoring a simmering sauce, and responding to conversational queries without losing track of any individual goal. However, current LLM architectures fundamentally lack this cognitive flexibility. When forced to interleave subtasks, these models suffer from catastrophic interference, a phenomenon where the context and rules of one task overwrite or corrupt the state of another, leading to critical failures in task completion and reasoning.

The core problem lies in a profound disconnect between cognitive science's understanding of human task switching and the architectures of autonomous AI agents. While cognitive science has extensively studied how the human brain manages executive control and goal maintenance (Kiesel et al., 2010), AI research has primarily focused on scaling model parameters or implementing rudimentary memory buffers. Existing approaches, such as the standard ReAct framework or basic Buffer of Thoughts (BoT) prompting, provide passive memory storage but lack active, algorithmic mechanisms for suppressing interference from inactive task contexts. This limitation is well-documented in recent surveys highlighting the architectural bottlenecks of agentic systems (Yiming et al., 2025). Consequently, when faced with complex, multi-task environments, LLM agents exhibit severe degradations in performance, characterized by attention dilution and context contamination.

To bridge this gap, this proposal introduces CogniSwitch, a novel cognitive-control-inspired architecture that directly translates human psychological frameworks of executive control into a computationally actionable model for LLM agents. The central innovation of CogniSwitch is its algorithmic "Control Signal" mechanism, which actively modulates the LLM's attention heads during inference to suppress interference from inactive task contexts. This is paired with an "Episodic Buffer of Thoughts," a structured memory module that maintains distinct, task-specific rule schemas. Unlike prior neuro-symbolic architectures that focus primarily on logical reasoning (Guizzardi, 2005), CogniSwitch targets the dynamic, moment-to-moment control of information flow, mimicking the human prefrontal cortex's ability to maintain and shift goal states.

The expected contributions of this research are threefold. First, it provides a biologically-inspired computational model that formally bridges cognitive psychology and AI agent architectures. Second, it offers empirical demonstrations of significantly reduced multi-task interference errors through a novel multi-task embodied text environment. Third, it establishes a new, standardized evaluation framework for assessing cognitive flexibility in artificial agents, addressing a critical gap in current LLM evaluation methodologies (Ras et al., 2022). The remainder of this proposal details the proposed method, evaluation plan, and risk mitigation strategies for realizing the CogniSwitch architecture.

## Related Work

**LLM Agent Architectures and Memory Management.** The field of LLM agents has evolved rapidly, moving from simple prompting techniques to complex, agentic frameworks. Comprehensive surveys by Yiming et al. (2025) detail how modern agents combine foundation models with environmental perception and tool-use capabilities. To manage the extensive context required for these tasks, methods like the Buffer of Thoughts (BoT) have been developed to maintain a passive repository of previous reasoning steps. Similarly, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) frameworks, such as those applied in personalized AI systems (Asai et al., 2023), allow agents to query external knowledge bases. However, these architectures primarily treat memory as a static resource. They lack active, algorithmic mechanisms for context segregation and interference suppression. When multiple tasks are interleaved, the passive nature of these memory buffers leads to attention dilution, as the LLM's context window becomes saturated with conflicting instructions and state information from various tasks. CogniSwitch addresses this limitation by introducing an active control signal that dynamically gates information flow, preventing the uncontrolled merging of distinct task contexts.

**Cognitive Task Switching and Executive Control.** In cognitive psychology, the task-switching paradigm has been instrumental in understanding human executive control. Research extensively documents how humans manage multiple goals, shift attention, and suppress interference from inactive tasks (Kiesel et al., 2010). This body of work establishes that human cognitive flexibility relies on active goal maintenance and inhibitory control mechanisms. Despite the rich theoretical frameworks in cognitive science, there is a profound disconnect in translating these biological principles into computational models for AI. Current AI systems do not possess an equivalent of the human prefrontal cortex's executive control. While some prior work has explored neuro-symbolic architectures for structural conceptual modeling (Guizzardi, 2005), these approaches focus on ontological foundations rather than the dynamic, moment-to-moment control of information flow. CogniSwitch bridges this gap by directly operationalizing the psychological constructs of 'goal maintenance' and 'inhibitory control' into a mathematical framework that modulates LLM attention.

**Embodied Agents and Reinforcement Learning.** The development of embodied AI agents, particularly in text-based environments, has leveraged Reinforcement Learning (RL) to optimize action sequences. Foundational surveys by Kaelbling et al. (1996) establish the core principles of RL in decision-making. More recently, methods like SG-Nav (Yin et al., 2024) have used 3D scene graphs to prompt LLMs for zero-shot object navigation, demonstrating the power of combining LLMs with structured environmental representations. Furthermore, the emergence of Agentic RL marks a shift toward training LLMs as autonomous agents. However, these RL-based approaches often treat multi-task management as a monolithic optimization problem, failing to explicitly isolate and manage distinct task contexts. They rely on the LLM's implicit reasoning to handle task interleaving, which frequently fails in complex scenarios. CogniSwitch differs by explicitly structuring the agent's internal state through the Episodic Buffer and using the Control Signal to algorithmically direct the LLM's focus, thereby providing a more robust and interpretable mechanism for multi-task control than end-to-end RL training.

## Proposed Method

The CogniSwitch architecture introduces a biologically-inspired computational model for mitigating catastrophic interference in multi-task LLM agents. The method translates the cognitive psychology frameworks of executive control and goal maintenance into a structured, algorithmic intervention applied during the LLM's inference process. The architecture consists of two primary components: the Episodic Buffer of Thoughts (EBoT) and the Control Signal Mechanism (CSM).

**Formal Problem Definition**

Consider an autonomous LLM agent operating within a complex environment. Let the global task be defined as a set of $N$ interleaved subtasks, denoted as $\mathcal{T} = \{T_1, T_2, \dots, T_N\}$. At any given discrete time step $t$, the agent receives an environmental observation $o_t$ and must select an action $a_t$ from a composite action space. The agent maintains a global context history $\mathcal{H}_t = \{(o_1, a_1), \dots, (o_{t-1}, a_{t-1})\}$. 

In standard LLM agents, the probability of selecting the next action is modeled as:
$$P(a_t | o_t, \mathcal{H}_t) = \text{LLM}(o_t, \mathcal{H}_t)$$
This formulation is highly susceptible to catastrophic interference. When $\mathcal{H}_t$ contains interleaved state-action pairs from multiple distinct subtasks, the LLM's attention mechanism computes pairwise relationships across all tokens, regardless of task boundaries. This leads to attention dilution and context contamination, where the rules and states of an inactive task $T_j$ improperly influence the action selection for the currently active task $T_i$.

**Episodic Buffer of Thoughts (EBoT)**

The EBoT extends standard context management by maintaining distinct, task-specific rule schemas. Instead of a single monolithic context history $\mathcal{H}_t$, the EBoT partitions the history into task-specific episodic memories. Let $\mathcal{M}_t = \{M_1, M_2, \dots, M_N\}$ represent the set of active memory buffers at time $t$, where each $M_i$ corresponds to subtask $T_i$. Each memory buffer $M_i$ contains the relevant observation-action history and a specific rule schema $R_i$ defining the constraints and goals of $T_i$. 

When the agent receives a new observation $o_t$, a routing function $\phi(o_t)$ first classifies the observation to its corresponding subtask $T_k$. The observation is then appended to the corresponding memory buffer: $M_k \leftarrow M_k \cup \{o_t\}$. The inactive memory buffers remain isolated, preserving their specific contextual integrity without cross-contamination.

**Control Signal Mechanism (CSM)**

The CSM is the core algorithmic innovation, translating the cognitive concept of inhibitory control into a mathematical operation on the LLM's attention heads. When the agent must generate an action for the active task $T_k$, the CSM generates a control signal vector $\mathbf{c}_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ that modulates the LLM's attention computation.

For a standard multi-head attention layer, the attention weights are typically computed as:
$$\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}_i^\top \mathbf{k}_j}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)}{\sum_{m=1}^{L} \exp\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}_i^\top \mathbf{k}_m}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)}$$
where $\mathbf{q}_i$ is the query vector for token $i$, $\mathbf{k}_j$ is the key vector for token $j$, $d_k$ is the dimension of the key vectors, and $L$ is the sequence length.

CogniSwitch introduces a task-specific suppression mask $\mathbf{s}_k \in \mathbb{R}^L$, where $s_{k, j} = 0$ if token $j$ belongs to an inactive task $T_j \neq T_k$, and $s_{k, j} = 1$ otherwise. The modified attention computation becomes:
$$\tilde{\alpha}_{ij} = \frac{\exp\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}_i^\top \mathbf{k}_j}{\sqrt{d_k}} + \log s_{k, j}\right)}{\sum_{m=1}^{L} \exp\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}_i^\top \mathbf{k}_m}{\sqrt{d_k}} + \log s_{k, m}\right)}$$

This formulation algorithmically suppresses the attention weights assigned to tokens from inactive tasks, effectively preventing interference. The control signal vector $\mathbf{c}_k$ is generated by a lightweight, trainable routing network $f_\theta$ that takes the current observation $o_t$ and a summary of the task schemas as input: $\mathbf{c}_k = f_\theta(o_t, \{R_1, \dots, R_N\})$.

**Algorithmic Steps**

1. **Initialization**: For a given global task $\mathcal{T}$, initialize the EBoT with task-specific rule schemas $\{R_1, \dots, R_N\}$ and empty memory buffers $\mathcal{M}_0$.
2. **Observation Routing**: At time $t$, receive observation $o_t$. Use the routing function $\phi(o_t)$ to classify the observation to subtask $T_k$.
3. **Memory Update**: Update the active memory buffer: $M_k \leftarrow M_k \cup \{o_t\}$.
4. **Control Signal Generation**: Generate the suppression mask $\mathbf{s}_k$ via the routing network $f_\theta$.
5. **Attention Modulation**: Forward the active memory buffer $M_k$ and the global context through the LLM, applying the modified attention computation $\tilde{\alpha}_{ij}$ at each specified layer.
6. **Action Generation**: Sample action $a_t$ from the modulated LLM output distribution.
7. **Iteration**: Append $(o_t, a_t)$ to $M_k$ and proceed to time $t+1$.

This bridges the critical cross-domain gap identified between cognitive psychology and artificial intelligence, providing a foundational blueprint for future bio-inspired agent designs.
2. **Empirical Demonstration of Reduced Multi-Task Interference**: Through the novel CogniSwitch architecture, this work will provide concrete empirical evidence of significant reductions in catastrophic interference and context-switching errors. ## Evaluation Plan

**Datasets**
The evaluation will utilize a novel, custom-built environment called the **CogniHome** text-based embodied simulation. CogniHome is designed to rigorously test multi-task interleaving and cognitive flexibility. The environment simulates a dynamic household where the agent must manage concurrent responsibilities, such as cooking a multi-step recipe, answering intermittent trivia questions, and monitoring a simulated baby. The primary dataset is **CogniHome-v1**, containing 500 distinct scenarios with an average of 3.5 interleaved subtasks per scenario. Each scenario provides a gold-standard trajectory of actions, subtask boundaries, and goal states. The environment is implemented as a Python package and will be made available at a dedicated GitHub repository (e.g., `github.com/cogniswitch-env/cognihome`). We will also evaluate on the **ALFWorld** dataset (Shridhar et al., 2020), a standard text-based embodied environment, by modifying its tasks to introduce forced interleaving, thereby providing a point of comparison with existing embodied agent literature.

**Baselines**
The proposed CogniSwitch architecture will be compared against the following baselines:
1. **ReAct** (Yao et al., 2023): A standard prompting baseline where the LLM reasons and acts in an interleaved manner without any explicit memory management or context control.
2. **Buffer of Thoughts (BoT)** (Yang et al., 2024): A method that uses a passive memory buffer to store previous reasoning steps, representing the current state-of-the-art in context management for LLM agents.
3. **Reflexion** (Shinn et al., 2023): An RL-based approach that equips agents with linguistic self-reflection to learn from previous failures, providing a comparison against RL-based context management.
4. **CogniSwitch (No-CSM)**: An ablation baseline using the CogniSwitch architecture but without the Control Signal Mechanism, relying solely on the EBoT for context partitioning.

**Metrics**
We define the following metrics for a rigorous evaluation:
1. **Overall Task Success Rate (TSR)**: The percentage of scenarios in which the agent successfully completes all $N$ subtasks within the designated step limit. $TSR = \frac{\text{Number of successful scenarios}}{\text{Total scenarios}} \times 100$.
2. **Cognitive Interference Error Rate (CIER)**: A novel metric quantifying the frequency of errors caused by cross-task contamination. An action is classified as a cognitive interference error if it applies the rules, objects, or logic of an inactive task $T_j$ to the currently active task $T_k$. $CIER = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb{1}[\text{error}(a_t) \text{ is interference}]}{\text{Total actions}}$.
3. **Context-Switching Accuracy (CSA)**: Measures the agent's ability to correctly resume an interrupted task. After a context switch from $T_i$ to $T_j$ and back to $T_i$, CSA measures if the agent's first action correctly follows the state of $T_i$ prior to interruption. $CSA = \frac{\text{Correct resumption actions}}{\text{Total context switches}}$.
4. **Multi-Task Efficiency (MTE)**: The ratio of the optimal number of steps to the actual steps taken by the agent. $MTE = \frac{\text{Optimal steps for all subtasks}}{\text{Actual steps taken}}$.

**Ablation Design**
To isolate the contributions of individual components, we propose the following ablation experiments:
1. **Impact of the Control Signal Mechanism (CSM)**: We will compare the full CogniSwitch model against the "CogniSwitch (No-CSM)" baseline. This isolates the effect of the active attention modulation from the passive memory partitioning provided by the EBoT.
2. **Routing Function Architecture**: We will evaluate different implementations of the task routing function $\phi(o_t)$, comparing a fine-tuned BERT-based classifier, a simple LLM-based prompting approach, and a heuristic keyword matcher, to understand the robustness required for accurate task routing.
3. **Suppression Mask Granularity**: We will analyze the effect of applying the suppression mask $\mathbf{s}_k$ at different granularities: (a) at the full sequence level, (b) at the sentence level, and (c) at the token level, to determine the optimal balance between interference suppression and information retention.

## Risk Mitigation

**Risk 1: Computational Prohibitivity of Attention Modulation.** The core proposal to dynamically re-weight internal LLM attention heads via a control signal during inference may be computationally prohibitive, requiring modifications at the architectural level of large models.
*   **Mitigation**: We will initially implement and validate CogniSwitch using smaller, open-source LLMs (e.g., Llama-3-8B, Mistral-7B) where attention manipulation is more tractable. We will leverage efficient attention masking techniques compatible with FlashAttention to minimize overhead.
*   **Fallback Plan**: If direct attention head manipulation proves infeasible, we will pivot to a "soft" control signal implemented via a prompting-based routing mechanism. Instead of modifying attention weights directly, the CSM will dynamically construct the LLM's input prompt at each step, explicitly including only the active task's memory buffer and rule schema, thereby achieving context segregation at the input level rather than the architectural level.

**Risk 2: Conflation of Cognitive Interference with Standard Hallucinations.** Isolating "cognitive interference errors" from standard LLM hallucinations or basic context window limitations in the CogniHome environment will be methodologically challenging.
*   **Mitigation**: We will design CogniHome scenarios with highly distinct subtask domains (e.g., a cooking task vs. a trivia task) to make cross-task contamination obvious. The CIER metric will be evaluated through a rigorous manual annotation process by domain experts, supplemented by automated checks for the presence of inactive task vocabulary in active task actions.
*   **Fallback Plan**: If precise isolation proves elusive, we will reframe the evaluation to focus on holistic multi-task performance improvements (TSR, MTE) and qualitative case studies, demonstrating the overall utility of the CogniSwitch architecture without relying solely on the granular CIER metric.

**Risk 3: Abstractness of Cognitive Translations.** Translating psychological theories of "goal maintenance" into a functional neuro-symbolic algorithm may prove too abstract to yield significant empirical improvements over simpler heuristic-based context management prompting techniques.
*   **Mitigation**: We will ensure rigorous ablation studies comparing CogniSwitch against strong heuristic-based baselines. The mathematical formalization of the control signal will be iteratively refined based on empirical feedback, ensuring the cognitive translation is grounded in computational practicality.
*   **Fallback Plan**: Should the full cognitive framework fail to outperform heuristics, the research will contribute a valuable negative result, analyzing the limitations of direct cognitive-to-AI translation. We will then distill the most effective components of CogniSwitch (e.g., the EBoT memory partitioning) into a simplified, practical prompting framework for multi-task agents.

It entirely lacks technical depth, methodological specifications, and evaluation details, making it currently impossible to assess its feasibility or potential impact. To succeed, the proposal requires substantial expansion to translate its strong theoretical foundation into concrete, reproducible algorithms and rigorous experimental designs.
