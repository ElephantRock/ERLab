# Rank Stability of Robust Regression under Seeded Covariate Perturbation: A Controlled Study of Ridge and Huber Regression on Airfoil Self-Noise and Concrete Strength

## Abstract

Robust regression estimators are routinely compared on clean benchmark data, yet deployed tabular models frequently face corrupted, noisy, or drifted covariates at inference time. This paper presents a controlled, fully deterministic empirical study (capability `tabular_robust_regression_v1`) of rank stability in robust regression on two executed tabular regression datasets, `concrete_strength` and `airfoil_self_noise`. Two estimators—ridge regression solved in closed form (lambda = 1.0) and Huber regression fitted by 50 iterations of iteratively reweighted least squares initialized from the ridge solution—are evaluated against a training-set mean-predictor baseline. Seeded zero-mean Gaussian noise is injected into standardized test features at four severities (0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75), and MAE, RMSE, and R-squared are recorded per (severity, method). The executed results show that rankings are metric-dependent: ridge attains lower RMSE and higher R-squared than Huber at every severity on both datasets (e.g., RMSE 10.895576 [RESULT-33] versus 10.995134 [RESULT-30] at severity 0.0 on `concrete_strength`), whereas the MAE ranking reverses on both datasets—by severity 0.25 on `airfoil_self_noise` (3.741122 [RESULT-10] versus 3.742593 [RESULT-7]) and at severity 0.75 on `concrete_strength` (9.212387 [RESULT-49] versus 9.327266 [RESULT-46]). Ridge MAE rises from 3.691782 [RESULT-4] to 4.392018 [RESULT-22] on `airfoil_self_noise` while remaining below the baseline of 5.524907 [RESULT-25]. Because the RMSE/R-squared versus MAE asymmetry is partly predictable from loss geometry, the contribution is deliberately bounded: a frozen, exactly reproducible severity-sweep protocol and a complete 54-metric record, not a new estimator. The findings caution against single-metric model selection under input perturbation.

## 1. Introduction

Regression on tabular data is among the most mature areas of applied machine learning, and linear-type estimators remain widely deployed on engineering and scientific datasets. In deployment, the covariates fed to such models are rarely pristine: sensors drift, measurements are corrupted, and test-time input distributions differ from training distributions. Despite this maturity, a basic evaluative question remains open: when the severity of covariate perturbation increases in a controlled fashion, are method rankings stable, or do they reverse? Leaderboards and benchmark reports typically fix a single evaluation condition and a single panel of metrics, so a ranking established under clean test covariates may silently fail to transfer to perturbed ones. Yet the choice of metric is itself contested: peer discussion of regression evaluation argues about the relative informativeness of R-squared versus MAE, MSE, and RMSE [SOURCE-4], and multi-metric reporting is common practice [SOURCE-2], [SOURCE-8]. If two estimators trade leadership across MAE, RMSE, and R-squared as perturbation severity grows, then a leaderboard computed at one severity under one metric can be misleading without any error on the practitioner's part. Moreover, evaluations that depend on external machine-learning libraries with opaque defaults compromise exact reproduction—an acute concern when the quantities being compared differ by fractions of a percent.

Existing work leaves this question unaddressed along several axes. Lines of research on distributional shift adapt predictions or validity guarantees to covariate discrepancy—for example, conformal prediction sets for tabular regression under covariate shift [SOURCE-1] and their extensions to gradient-boosted models [SOURCE-3]—but they do not sweep perturbation severity as a controlled dose–response axis for estimator ranking. The robust-regression literature (reviewed as background in Section 2) compares estimators predominantly under training-side contamination or heavy-tailed residuals [SOURCE-22], not under inference-time corruption of clean fitted models. Comparative evaluation studies rank regression models by R², RMSE, and runtime, typically at a single noise level [SOURCE-30]. None of these strands measures cross-metric rank agreement itself as a function of perturbation severity.

This paper addresses the gap with a controlled dose–response study built around a frozen method contract, executed as capability `tabular_robust_regression_v1` on `concrete_strength` (the primary dataset of record) and `airfoil_self_noise`. The research question is stated precisely: are robust-regression method rankings stable as covariate perturbation severity increases, or do rank reversals occur in MAE, RMSE, and R² across tabular regression datasets? The study fits ridge and Huber regression on training-standardized features of the two datasets, perturbs only the standardized test covariates with deterministic seeded Gaussian noise at severities 0.0, 0.25, 0.5, and 0.75, and compares both estimators against a mean-predictor baseline whose predictions do not read the covariates and are therefore identical at every severity. All solvers are implemented without external machine-learning libraries, so every number reported is exactly reproducible from the recorded seeds.

The contributions are deliberately bounded to the executed analysis. First, the paper contributes a frozen, deterministic severity-sweep protocol for robust-regression evaluation, with the executed method contract reproduced verbatim. Second, it documents a complete 54-metric factorial record (2 datasets × 2 estimators × 4 severities × 3 metrics, plus severity-invariant baselines) with per-claim result provenance. Third, it establishes an empirical finding within that record: RMSE and R² rankings are stable across all tested severities on both datasets (ridge leads RMSE at all eight severity–dataset cells, e.g., 10.895576 [RESULT-33] versus 10.995134 [RESULT-30] on `concrete_strength` at severity 0.0), while MAE rankings undergo dataset-dependent reversals (3.741122 [RESULT-10] versus 3.742593 [RESULT-7] on `airfoil_self_noise` at severity 0.25; 9.212387 [RESULT-49] versus 9.327266 [RESULT-46] on `concrete_strength` at severity 0.75). Fourth, it analyzes dose–response magnitudes, a non-monotonicity in MAE at high severity, and the persistent gap to the covariate-blind baseline, drawing practical lessons for metric selection and deployment safety margins. What this paper does *not* contribute is equally explicit: no new estimator is proposed; stronger robust baselines such as LAD, quantile, RANSAC, and Theil–Sen were not executed and are labeled future work; the hand-rolled solvers were not validated against reference implementations; and no statistical tests accompany the single-run point estimates. Because ridge minimizes a quadratic loss and Huber's linear tail approximates an absolute-error loss, the clean-condition cross-metric disagreement is partly predictable from loss geometry; the non-obvious empirical content is the *severity location* of the MAE reversals and the *persistence* of the squared-error margins, which are measured rather than assumed.

## 2. Related Work

**Covariate shift in tabular regression.** Work on conformal prediction sets for tabular regression under covariate shift provides finite-sample coverage guarantees when the test covariate distribution differs from the calibration distribution [SOURCE-1], with extended guarantees for gradient-boosted models [SOURCE-3]. That line adapts predictions or validity guarantees to distributional discrepancy. The present study is complementary: rather than correcting for shift, it *injects* shift of controlled magnitude into test covariates and measures how estimator rankings respond, providing the severity axis along which such corrections would need to be stress-tested. Robustness-oriented evaluations that perturb inputs at test time form a family of corruption protocols of varying sophistication; the protocol here instantiates the simplest member of this family—additive, seeded, homoscedastic Gaussian corruption applied to standardized features at inference time—which isolates the effect of perturbation severity without confounding from retraining.

**Robust regression estimators (background; not executed here).** As background and motivation, robust regression has been scaled to large-data problems, extended through the TELBS method, formulated as mixture-of-distributions models, developed as robust rank regression for life-function estimation, and studied as distributed high-dimensional rank regression that avoids moment assumptions under heavy-tailed noise; robust multiple regression also appears in domain systems such as distant-talking speech recognition [SOURCE-22]. These estimator families—along with LAD, quantile, RANSAC, and Theil–Sen, which likewise were not executed in this study—primarily address training-side contamination or heavy-tailed residuals. They are cited here as motivation for the broader estimator pool that the frozen two-estimator contract deliberately excludes; extending the pool is future work, not a demonstrated contribution of this paper.

**Evaluation metrics and model ranking.** The informativeness of R-squared relative to MAE, MSE, and RMSE is actively debated [SOURCE-4], and multi-metric panels (RMSE, MSE, MAE, MAPE) are standard in regression evaluation [SOURCE-2], [SOURCE-8]. Comparative studies rank regression models by R², RMSE, and runtime, or by RMSE across models, typically at a single noise level [SOURCE-30]. This paper treats cross-metric rank agreement itself as the object of study, as a function of perturbation severity—an axis absent from the single-condition comparisons above.

**Reference baselines.** Applied tabular modeling commonly reports simple structural reference points against which learned models are judged [SOURCE-24]. The mean predictor used here is exactly such a reference, with the special property of severity invariance; Section 7 discusses why it is a floor rather than a sufficient baseline set.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Problem definition

Let a dataset $D = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ consist of feature vectors $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^p$ and continuous targets $y_i \in \mathbb{R}$. A deterministic seeded shuffle (seed 42) partitions row indices into a training set $T$ (80%) and a test set $S$ (20%). Features are standardized using training-split statistics only, giving standardized vectors $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i$. Two estimators are fitted on $T$ and evaluated on $S$ under perturbations of the standardized test covariates. The research question is whether the ranking of estimators by MAE, RMSE, and $R^2$ on $S$ is stable across perturbation severities $s \in \{0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75\}$.

### 3.2 Executed protocol (frozen method contract)

The following executed protocol of capability `tabular_robust_regression_v1` is reproduced verbatim as the frozen method contract:

> - Two regression models are fitted on features standardized with training-set statistics. Ridge regression is solved in closed form (lambda = 1.0, with the intercept left unregularized) by pure-Python Gaussian elimination. Huber regression is fitted by iteratively reweighted least squares initialized from the ridge solution, with delta = 1.345 times a MAD-based residual scale (1.4826 x median absolute deviation about the residual median, floored at 1e-8) recomputed each iteration, for exactly 50 iterations. No external machine-learning library is used.
> - The baseline is the training-set mean predictor. Its predictions do not read the covariates, so its MAE, RMSE, and R-squared are reported once per dataset and are identical at every perturbation severity.
> - Covariate perturbation adds deterministic seeded zero-mean Gaussian noise to the standardized test features only, with standard deviation equal to severity x 0.5 at severities 0.0, 0.25, 0.5, and 0.75 (seed 42 plus the severity index, Box-Muller sampling). Labels are never perturbed.
> - MAE and RMSE (lower is better) and R-squared (R2 = 1 - SS_res/SS_tot with SS_tot taken about the test-set mean; higher is better) are computed per (severity, method) on the perturbed test set.
> - The data are split by a deterministic seeded shuffle (seed 42) into 80% training and 20% test rows; standardization statistics come from the training split only.

### 3.3 Estimators

**Ridge regression.** With intercept $\beta_0$ left unregularized and $\lambda = 1.0$,

$$
\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} \;=\; \arg\min_{\beta_0, \boldsymbol\beta} \sum_{i \in T}\bigl(y_i - \beta_0 - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i^\top \boldsymbol\beta\bigr)^2 + \lambda \|\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2 .
$$

After absorbing the intercept via centering, the normal equations $(\tilde{X}^\top \tilde{X} + \lambda I)\boldsymbol\beta = \tilde{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$ are solved by pure-Python Gaussian elimination, eliminating any dependence on external linear-algebra backends.

**Huber regression.** Huber regression is fitted by iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) initialized from the ridge solution. At each iteration, the residual scale is recomputed as

$$
\hat\sigma = \max\!\Bigl(10^{-8},\; 1.4826 \times \operatorname{median}_i \bigl| r_i - \operatorname{median}_j r_j \bigr|\Bigr), \qquad \delta = 1.345\, \hat\sigma,
$$

and the weights $w_i = \min(1, \delta/|r_i|)$ define the weighted least-squares update. Exactly 50 iterations are executed, with no early stopping, so the fit is a deterministic function of the training split.

**Mean-predictor baseline.** The baseline predicts $\hat{y} = \bar{y}_T$ (the training-set mean target) for every test row. Because it does not read the covariates, its metrics are invariant to any covariate perturbation, serving as a severity-invariant reference line analogous to structural baselines used in applied tabular modeling [SOURCE-24].

### 3.4 Perturbation model and metrics

At severity $s$, each standardized test vector is corrupted as

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}_i = \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i + \boldsymbol\epsilon^{(s)}_i, \qquad \boldsymbol\epsilon^{(s)}_i \sim \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\mathbf{0},\, (0.5\, s)^2 I\bigr),
$$

with draws generated by Box–Muller sampling under seed $42 + \text{severity index}$. Labels are never perturbed, and training data are never perturbed; the protocol therefore measures inference-time robustness of already-fitted models, not retraining under shift. Predictions $\hat{y}^{(s)}_i$ from each fitted model are evaluated by

$$
\mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i \in S} \bigl| y_i - \hat{y}^{(s)}_i \bigr|, \quad
\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i \in S} \bigl( y_i - \hat{y}^{(s)}_i \bigr)^2}, \quad
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i \in S} (y_i - \hat{y}^{(s)}_i)^2}{\sum_{i \in S} (y_i - \bar{y}_{S})^2},
$$

with $\bar{y}_S$ the test-set mean.

### 3.5 Scope delimitation of the methodology

Three methodological delimitations must be stated explicitly because they bound all claims. First, the pure-Python solvers were not cross-validated against reference implementations (e.g., standard library ridge/Huber fits); exact reproducibility is guaranteed by the frozen contract, but numerical agreement with external implementations is not an executed result. Second, only ridge and Huber were executed; LAD, quantile, RANSAC, Theil–Sen, and the robust families cited as background [SOURCE-22] are future work. Third, no hyperparameter ablations over $\lambda$, $\delta$, iteration count, or noise family were executed, and no statistical significance tests accompany the point estimates; the executed grid is exactly the one described in Section 4.

## 4. Experimental Design

**Datasets.** Two tabular regression datasets were executed: `airfoil_self_noise` (experiment_result_id=1), a physical-acoustics regression task in which aerodynamic and geometric blade attributes predict a continuous sound-pressure response, and `concrete_strength` (experiment_result_id=2), a civil-engineering regression task in which mixture composition and age attributes predict continuous compressive strength in MPa. `concrete_strength` is the primary dataset of record for this experiment. No dataset other than these two was executed.

**Grid.** The design is factorial: 2 datasets × 2 estimators (ridge, Huber) × 4 severities (0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75) × 3 metrics (MAE, RMSE, R²), plus one severity-invariant mean-predictor baseline per dataset scored on the same 3 metrics, yielding 54 recorded metrics. The primary metric declared by the capability is `0_0_ridge_mae` (ridge MAE at severity 0.0, lower is better), anchoring the clean-condition comparison: 3.691782 [RESULT-4] on `airfoil_self_noise` and 8.639468 [RESULT-31] on `concrete_strength`.

**Baselines and metrics rationale.** The mean predictor isolates the value of reading covariates at all; its severity invariance makes it the only fixed reference line across the sweep. Its adequacy is deliberately limited: it is a sanity floor, not a competitive baseline, and the ranking claims of this paper are therefore pairwise (ridge versus Huber) rather than leaderboard claims over a broad estimator pool. Ablations over $\lambda$, $\delta$, iteration count, noise family, and statistical testing across seeds were designed as future extensions and were not executed. No hyperparameter tuning is performed: $\lambda = 1.0$, $\delta = 1.345\hat\sigma$, and the 50-iteration budget are fixed by the contract, removing tuning as a confound.

**Analysis plan.** For each dataset and metric, the analysis asks (i) whether the estimator ranking is identical at all four severities (rank stability), (ii) whether metrics agree with each other at each severity (cross-metric agreement), (iii) whether degradation from severity 0.0 to 0.75 is monotone, and (iv) how far each estimator remains from the severity-invariant baseline. This plan treats the severity sweep as the ablation axis of the study.

### 5.3 Cross-dataset rank stability

Aggregating across the executed results, a clear asymmetry emerges. **RMSE and R² rankings are perfectly stable:** ridge outranks Huber at all four severities on both datasets (eight of eight cells per metric). **MAE rankings are unstable in a dataset-dependent way:** the reversal occurs between severities 0.0 and 0.25 on `airfoil_self_noise` (from 3.666145 [RESULT-1] versus 3.691782 [RESULT-4] to 3.741122 [RESULT-10] versus 3.742593 [RESULT-7]) and only at severity 0.75 on `concrete_strength` (from 8.517702 [RESULT-28] versus 8.639468 [RESULT-31] to 9.212387 [RESULT-49] versus 9.327266 [RESULT-46]). In both datasets, Huber's clean-data MAE advantage does not survive the largest tested perturbation. The baseline is uniformly last on MAE and RMSE and trivially near zero on R² (5.524907 [RESULT-25], 6.802082 [RESULT-27], -0.006701 [RESULT-26] on `airfoil_self_noise`; 13.773691 [RESULT-52], 16.901381 [RESULT-54], -0.012442 [RESULT-53] on `concrete_strength`), confirming that both estimators extract covariate signal that degrades gradually rather than collapsing, even at severity 0.75 where noise standard deviation reaches 0.375 on features standardized to unit training variance.

### 5.4 What is and is not predictable from loss geometry

Part of the observed pattern is close to structural. Ridge minimizes a purely quadratic loss, so its fitted coefficients are aligned with squared-error evaluation; Huber's loss grows linearly beyond $\delta$, approximating an absolute-error loss, so its clean-condition advantage on MAE (3.666145 [RESULT-1] versus 3.691782 [RESULT-4]; 8.517702 [RESULT-28] versus 8.639468 [RESULT-31]) and disadvantage on RMSE/R² are consistent with the loss geometry rather than surprising. What the geometry does *not* predict is the empirical content this study contributes: the specific severity at which each dataset's MAE ranking reverses, the near-zero reversal margin of 0.001471 on `airfoil_self_noise` (3.741122 [RESULT-10] versus 3.742593 [RESULT-7]), the 0.114879 reversal margin at severity 0.75 on `concrete_strength` (9.212387 [RESULT-49] versus 9.327266 [RESULT-46]), the non-monotonic MAE movement between severities 0.5 and 0.75 on `concrete_strength`, and the persistence of ridge's RMSE/R² margins at every tested severity. These are measured facts about two specific estimator implementations on two specific datasets under a specific seeded perturbation; no generalization beyond them is claimed.

## 6. Expected Results

This section states the hypotheses that motivated the design, distinguishes them from the observed outcomes in Section 5, and records expectations for regimes beyond the executed grid; all forward-looking statements here are hypothesized, not observed.

Three expectations were formulated a priori. First, all estimator metrics were expected to degrade monotonically with severity while remaining above the severity-invariant baseline. This was largely observed (e.g., ridge MAE from 3.691782 [RESULT-4] to 4.392018 [RESULT-22] on `airfoil_self_noise`, against a constant baseline of 5.524907 [RESULT-25]), with the exception of the MAE non-monotonicity at severity 0.75 on `concrete_strength` (9.55704 [RESULT-40] to 9.327266 [RESULT-46]; 9.578703 [RESULT-43] to 9.212387 [RESULT-49]), which the design had not anticipated. Second, because Huber's downweighting targets large *residuals* rather than corrupted *inputs*, ridge was expected to retain any squared-error advantage under input noise; this was observed on both datasets, where ridge led RMSE and R² at every severity (e.g., 4.67722 [RESULT-6] versus 4.717558 [RESULT-3]; 10.895576 [RESULT-33] versus 10.995134 [RESULT-30]). Third, MAE rankings were expected to be the fragile ones, since Huber's advantage at the clean point rested on small margins; this was confirmed by reversals on both datasets.

For regimes not executed, the expectations remain qualitative hypotheses. At severities beyond 0.75, the injected noise standard deviation approaches the scale of the standardized features themselves, and both estimators are expected to converge toward baseline-level MAE and RMSE with R² approaching zero. Multiple seeds per severity are expected to convert the tiny reversal margins into distributions whose overlap is quantifiable; the single-seed margins documented here (0.001471 on `airfoil_self_noise`) motivate that unexecuted analysis. Finally, additional tabular datasets (not executed in this study) are expected to exhibit MAE rank reversals at dataset-specific severities, as the two executed datasets already do at different points; no pooled or aggregate statistic is claimed across datasets.

## 7. Discussion

**Limitations.** The scope of every claim in this paper is bounded by the executed grid. Only two datasets, two linear estimators, one split per dataset, and one seed per severity were executed, and the estimator margins—while consistently signed for RMSE and R²—are small in absolute terms, so rank reversals at other seeds cannot be ruled out; no confidence intervals or statistical significance tests accompany the point estimates, and the near-zero MAE reversal margin on `airfoil_self_noise` (3.741122 [RESULT-10] versus 3.742593 [RESULT-7]) should be read as fragility evidence rather than a robust ordering. The only baseline is the mean predictor, which serves as a severity-invariant floor but not as a competitive reference; validation of the hand-rolled pure-Python solvers against reference implementations, additional robust estimators (LAD, quantile, RANSAC, Theil–Sen), ablations over $\lambda$, $\delta$, iteration count, and noise family, and multi-seed statistical testing are all unexecuted future work. No runtime or memory measurements are reported here, and none should be inferred. The Huber threshold $\delta$ is tied to a MAD-based residual scale recomputed each iteration, so its effective value is data-dependent; this is standard practice but means the estimator is not a fixed hyperparameter configuration across datasets. Finally, no theoretical analysis is offered; as Section 5.4 argues, the clean-condition cross-metric disagreement is largely predictable from loss geometry, and the study's value lies in the controlled measurement of where and by how much rankings change, not in a new estimator or a general theorem.

**Practical lessons.** First, model selection under input perturbation should not rely on a single metric: the MAE leaderboard and the RMSE/R² leaderboard disagree at the clean operating point on both datasets, and the MAE leaderboard reverses itself under perturbation. Second, the mean predictor provides a severity-invariant floor; both estimators retained large positive R² and large MAE margins over this floor at all tested severities (e.g., ridge MAE 9.212387 [RESULT-49] versus 13.773691 [RESULT-52] on `concrete_strength` at severity 0.75), suggesting that moderate standardized input noise (up to 0.375 standard deviations) is survivable for linear estimators on these tasks.

**Broader impact and ethics.** Both datasets concern physical and engineering systems with no personal or sensitive attributes, and the fully deterministic, library-free protocol lowers barriers to independent verification. A potential negative consequence of rank-stability findings is overgeneralization: practitioners might transfer conclusions across datasets, metrics, or perturbation families where they do not hold. The dataset-dependent location of the MAE reversals documented here is a direct caution against such transfer.

## 8. Conclusion

This paper presented a controlled, fully deterministic empirical study of rank stability in robust regression under seeded covariate perturbation, implemented as the executed capability `tabular_robust_regression_v1` on `concrete_strength` and `airfoil_self_noise`. Using a frozen method contract—closed-form ridge regression ($\lambda = 1.0$), Huber regression by 50 IRLS iterations from the ridge initialization with $\delta = 1.345$ times a MAD-based scale, a severity-invariant mean-predictor baseline, and seeded Gaussian corruption of standardized test features at four severities—the study recorded a complete 54-metric grid with per-claim provenance. The central finding, attributable directly to this executed ridge-and-Huber comparison, is an asymmetry in rank stability: ridge dominated RMSE and R² at every severity on both datasets (e.g., 10.895576 [RESULT-33] versus 10.995134 [RESULT-30]), whereas MAE rankings reversed on both datasets at dataset-dependent severity points (3.741122 [RESULT-10] versus 3.742593 [RESULT-7]; 9.212387 [RESULT-49] versus 9.327266 [RESULT-46]), and Huber's clean-data MAE advantage (3.666145 [RESULT-1]; 8.517702 [RESULT-28]) did not survive the highest tested perturbation. A secondary finding is that absolute-error and squared-error metrics can move in opposite directions between adjacent severities due to severity-specific noise draws (9.55704 [RESULT-40] to 9.327266 [RESULT-46] alongside 12.069235 [RESULT-42] to 12.420413 [RESULT-48]). The contribution is bounded accordingly: a reproducible severity-sweep protocol and a fully provenanced empirical record, not a new estimator. Future work—explicitly not executed here—includes validating the pure-Python solvers against reference implementations, sweeping severities beyond 0.75, adding heavier-tailed and adversarial corruption families, incorporating training-side contamination, running multiple seeds with statistical tests, adding ablations over $\lambda$, $\delta$, and iteration count, enlarging the estimator pool with LAD, quantile, RANSAC, Theil–Sen, and TELBS-style, mixture, and rank-type robust methods [SOURCE-11], [SOURCE-16], [SOURCE-21], [SOURCE-22], and studying shift-corrected prediction schemes [SOURCE-1], [SOURCE-3] along the same severity axis, together with runtime and memory profiling as explicitly measured quantities rather than assumed ones.
