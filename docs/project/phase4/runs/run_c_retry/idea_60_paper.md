# Sentiment-Integrated Urban Climatology: Correlating Social Media Discourse with Physical Mitigation Metrics

## Abstract

Urban Heat Islands (UHI) pose a severe threat to environmental sustainability and public health in rapidly urbanizing regions. As climate change intensifies the frequency of extreme heat events, the implementation of mitigation strategies such as green-blue-grey infrastructure (GBGI) has become a central focus of urban planning , .  This paper proposes "Sentiment-Integrated Urban Climatology," a novel computational framework that bridges this gap by correlating geotagged social media discourse with high-resolution physical sensor data. By employing advanced Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques to quantify public sentiment regarding heat and infrastructure, we construct a dynamic "Social Thermal Index." We compare this index against traditional physical mitigation metrics using spatio-temporal regression models to identify disparities where physical cooling is present but perceived thermal comfort remains low. Our expected results demonstrate that integrating social sentiment reveals critical "perception gaps" in urban design, particularly in areas with high vegetation density but low accessibility or safety. ## Introduction

The convergence of rapid urbanization and anthropogenic climate change has exacerbated the Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect, making urban overheating a critical challenge for 21st-century city design [SOURCE-1], [SOURCE-3]. As projected by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), the significant shifts in climate variables will increase the vulnerability of urban populations to extreme heat, necessitating robust adaptation strategies , . [SOURCE-2] The health implications of this trend are profound; the Lancet Countdown has repeatedly highlighted the increasing risks of heat-related mortality and morbidity linked to fossil fuel-driven climate change [SOURCE-6], [SOURCE-9], [SOURCE-16]. In response, urban planners and climatologists have increasingly turned to nature-based solutions (NbS) and green-blue-grey infrastructure (GBGI), such as parks, wetlands, and reflective surfaces, to mitigate ambient temperatures , .  However, these approaches treat the city as a purely physical system, neglecting the socio-psychological dimension of thermal comfort.  There is a distinct lack of computational frameworks that can leverage the vast, unstructured data generated by city dwellers to assess the perceived effectiveness of mitigation strategies at scale.

To address this limitation, this paper proposes a methodology that integrates Natural Language Processing (NLP) with urban climatology. We utilize geotagged social media data as a proxy for public perception, analyzing the sentiment of discourse related to heat and urban infrastructure. By correlating these sentiment trends with physical sensor data (temperature, humidity, vegetation indices), we aim to identify areas where physical mitigation efforts do not align with public experience. This approach aligns with the growing call for climate-resilient urban development that links policy, governance, and design with the lived experiences of residents . [SOURCE-27] Furthermore, it leverages the emerging potential of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) in tackling climate change by filling high-impact gaps in urban adaptation research . [SOURCE-18]

The contributions of this paper are threefold: First, we introduce a novel "Social Thermal Index" that quantifies the perceived thermal environment derived from social media sentiment. Second, we present a spatio-temporal correlation model that identifies mismatches between physical cooling metrics and social sentiment. Third, we provide a framework for evaluating the co-benefits of urban design interventions, moving beyond biophysical metrics to include social well-being and accessibility [SOURCE-8]. This work paves the way for more humane and effective climate-resilient city design.

### Problem Formulation
We define the problem of identifying perception gaps in urban heat mitigation as a spatio-temporal correlation task. Let the urban domain be represented as a graph $G = (V, E)$, where $V$ denotes a set of spatial locations (e.g., grid cells or census tracts) and $E$ represents the spatial adjacency between them. For each location $v \in V$ and time step $t$, we observe a set of physical variables $\mathbf{X}_{v,t}$ and a social sentiment variable $y_{v,t}$.

The physical feature vector $\mathbf{X}_{v,t}$ includes standard mitigation metrics:
* Land Surface Temperature (LST)
* Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI)
* Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI)
* Normalized Difference Built-up Index (NDBI)
* Air Temperature and Humidity (from weather stations)

These indicators are established in the literature as primary drivers of urban thermal environments [SOURCE-21], [SOURCE-13].

The target variable $y_{v,t}$ represents the "Social Thermal Index" (STI), a normalized sentiment score derived from geotagged social media posts generated in location $v$ at time $t$. The goal is to learn a function $f$ such that $f(\mathbf{X}_{v,t}) \approx y_{v,t}$, and subsequently analyze the residual error $\epsilon_{v,t} = y_{v,t} - f(\mathbf{X}_{v,t})$. Large residuals indicate locations where the physical environment predicts a certain level of comfort (high NDVI, low LST) that does not align with the public sentiment (negative sentiment), or vice versa.

### Data Acquisition and Processing

#### Physical Data
Physical data is aggregated from remote sensing products and local meteorological stations.  These variables serve as the ground-truth for the physical state of the urban climate. #### Social Data and NLP Pipeline
We collect geotagged social media posts (e.g., from X/Twitter) containing keywords related to heat, infrastructure, and outdoor activities (e.g., "hot," "shade," "park," "uncomfortable"). The raw text undergoes a preprocessing pipeline including tokenization, stop-word removal, and lemmatization (internal reasoning).

To quantify sentiment, we employ a transformer-based deep learning architecture. For each spatio-temporal bin $(v,t)$, we aggregate the individual post scores to generate the STI:
$$ y_{v,t} = \frac{1}{N_{v,t}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{v,t}} s_i $$
where $N_{v,t}$ is the number of posts in location $v$ at time $t$. To mitigate noise, we apply a spatial smoothing kernel over the graph $G$.
 The GCN layer captures the spatial influence of heat and greenery (e.g., heat advection between adjacent grid cells), while the LSTM layer captures temporal dynamics (e.g., diurnal cycles or heat waves).

The model is trained to minimize the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the predicted sentiment and the observed STI:
$$ \mathcal{L} = \sum_{(v,t) \in \mathcal{D}} (y_{v,t} - \hat{y}_{v,t})^2 $$
where $\hat{y}_{v,t}$ is the model output.

Once the model is trained, we analyze the residuals $\epsilon_{v,t}$. We define a "Perception Gap" $\delta_v$ for a location $v$ as the average residual over a heat wave period:
$$ \delta_v = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \epsilon_{v,t} $$
A significant negative $\delta_v$ (where physical conditions predict comfort, but sentiment is negative) suggests a failure in urban design or accessibility—areas that are physically "cool" but socially "hot." A significant positive $\delta_v$ suggests areas where infrastructure is lacking yet the community is resilient or satisfied (internal reasoning).
 The study period covers the summer months (June–August) over three consecutive years to capture variability in heat events.

**Physical Dataset:** We utilize Landsat 8 imagery for LST and spectral indices (NDVI, NDWI, NDBI), processed using Google Earth Engine. **Social Dataset:** We utilize the streaming API to collect geotagged posts. The dataset is filtered for English language posts containing keywords associated with thermal comfort and urban infrastructure. The corpus is expected to contain several hundred thousand posts, providing a dense coverage of the urban area.

## Discussion

The proposed methodology offers a powerful new lens for evaluating urban heat mitigation, yet it is not without limitations. A primary concern is the demographic bias inherent in social media data.  If a neighborhood lacks social media activity, our model may fail to identify distress there. Future work might integrate additional data sources, such as 311 call logs or community surveys, to balance this bias and ensure equitable climate adaptation [SOURCE-2].

From an ethical standpoint, the use of geotagged social media raises privacy concerns. While the data is often public, aggregating it to infer local well-being could lead to surveillance concerns if misused. It is imperative that such tools be used to allocate resources for climate resilience (e.g., where to plant new trees or build cooling centers) rather than for punitive measures or commercial exploitation.

The broader impact of this work lies in its potential to transform urban planning from a purely technical exercise into a socially responsive practice. By identifying "Perception Gaps," city planners can move beyond simply maximizing green space and focus on the quality and accessibility of that space. This aligns with the framework for co-benefits of Nature-based Solutions, emphasizing that successful interventions might deliver multiple benefits for people and nature [SOURCE-8]. However, we must acknowledge that machine learning is not a silver bullet. While AI can optimize and identify patterns , the implementation of climate-resilient design requires robust governance and policy integration . [SOURCE-27] The "Sentiment-Integrated" framework should be viewed as a decision-support tool, providing high-resolution evidence to inform the critical, complex task of adapting cities to climate change.

## Conclusion

This paper presented "Sentiment-Integrated Urban Climatology," a novel framework that correlates social media discourse with physical mitigation metrics to evaluate the human-centric effectiveness of UHI interventions. By combining NLP-derived sentiment scores with geospatial climate data, we introduced a method to identify "Perception Gaps"—areas where physical cooling does not translate to perceived comfort. Our approach addresses the limitations of traditional remote sensing and CFD methods by incorporating the social dimension of urban microclimates.  Future work will focus on mitigating demographic data biases and integrating real-time sensor networks to create a dynamic, city-wide dashboard for climate resilience.
