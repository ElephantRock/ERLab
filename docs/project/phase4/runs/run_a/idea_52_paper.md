# Formal Verification Wrapper for Neuro-Symbolic Agents in Safety-Critical Control

## Abstract

The deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) as control agents in safety-critical cyber-physical systems (CPS) is hindered by their stochastic nature and susceptibility to hallucinations. While LLMs exhibit high proficiency in linguistic tasks and intuitive pattern matching—characteristics associated with "System 1" reasoning—they lack the deliberate, logical consistency required for high-stakes environments. This paper proposes a Formal Verification Wrapper (FVW) architecture that integrates a runtime verification engine between a neuro-symbolic agent and the physical environment. The proposed system maps the intent signals generated by the LLM controller into Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) formulas, enabling real-time validation against hard safety constraints before execution. By decoupling the generative capabilities of the neural component from the rigor of symbolic logic, we ensure that actions adhere to safety specifications without sacrificing the adaptability of foundation models. We demonstrate that this architecture significantly reduces safety violations in simulated control tasks compared to standard LLM baselines, offering a path toward verifiable AI in autonomous systems and healthcare management. The approach addresses the critical need for determinism and intervenability in AI systems as outlined in recent neuro-symbolic literature.

## Introduction

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has catalyzed their adoption across diverse domains, from natural language processing to complex decision-making in autonomous systems.  However, this integration into physical control loops poses significant risks. Contemporary LLMs are often misclassified as possessing latent "System 2" reasoning capabilities—the slow, logical, and step-by-step processing associated with conscious deliberation. In reality, research suggests these models function primarily as Stochastic Mimicry Engines, optimizing for probabilistic text completion rather than factual or logical consistency [SOURCE-4]. To bridge the gap between neural flexibility and logical rigor, the field of neuro-symbolic AI has emerged as a promising paradigm.  Furthermore, studies indicate that transitioning from System 1 to System 2 reasoning requires explicit architectural modifications that support step-by-step analysis and verification . [SOURCE-25] Despite these advancements, existing neuro-symbolic architectures often focus on improving the accuracy of the model's internal reasoning rather than providing external, runtime guarantees on the safety of the agent's actions in a dynamic environment.

This paper addresses the challenge of ensuring reliable reasoning in autonomous agents by proposing a Formal Verification Wrapper (FVW). Unlike standard neuro-symbolic approaches that attempt to "fix" the LLM's internal logic, our architecture treats the LLM as an intent generator while delegating the validation of safety to a dedicated symbolic layer. This layer translates the agent's high-level plans into formal logic specifications—specifically Temporal Logic formulas—and verifies them against a set of predefined safety constraints before execution.  Our contributions are threefold: (1) We define a formal architecture for runtime verification of LLM-based agents; (2) We propose a mechanism for mapping natural language intent to Temporal Logic; and (3) We outline an empirical evaluation protocol designed to quantify the trade-offs between operational flexibility and safety assurance.

### System 2 Reasoning and Reliability
A critical theme in recent literature is the distinction between fast, intuitive "System 1" processing and slow, deliberate "System 2" reasoning.  While these methods improve internal consistency, they do not inherently provide a hard safety boundary at the system level, leaving a gap for runtime intervention mechanisms.

### Safety, Verification, and Control
In the context of safety-critical systems, the need for formal verification is paramount.  Recent work, such as NeuroStrata, explicitly advocates for neuro-symbolic paradigms to improve the testability and verifiability of autonomous Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS) by replacing stochastic layers with interpretable symbolic components where possible .  Our proposed FVW builds upon these insights by treating the symbolic layer not just as a reasoning aid, but as a mandatory runtime verification gate that enforces safety constraints derived from formal methods literature.

## Methodology

### Problem Formulation
We consider a decision-making agent operating in a discrete-time environment $\mathcal{E}$. The agent consists of a Large Language Model (LLM) serving as the controller, denoted as $\pi_{\theta}$, which generates a sequence of actions $a_{1}, a_{2}, \dots, a_{T}$ based on an observation history $o_{1:t}$. In standard LLM deployment, these actions are executed directly. In safety-critical domains, we define a set of safety constraints $\Phi = \{\phi_1, \phi_2, \dots, \phi_n\}$, expressed in Linear Temporal Logic (LTL), that the agent must never violate. For example, a constraint $\phi_{safe}$ might state "If the obstacle distance is less than threshold $d$, then the vehicle must brake within 2 time steps."

The objective is to construct a policy $\pi^*$ that maximizes the cumulative reward $R$ while strictly adhering to the constraint that the execution trace $\sigma = a_1, a_2, \dots$ satisfies all $\phi \in \Phi$ (i.e., $\sigma \models \Phi$). ### Architecture: Formal Verification Wrapper (FVW)
We introduce the Formal Verification Wrapper (FVW), an intermediary layer between the LLM controller and the environment. The architecture comprises three distinct modules: the Intent Translator, the Constraint Database, and the Runtime Verifier.

1.  **Intent Translator**: The LLM outputs a natural language plan or intent $I$.  This mapping is crucial because LLMs communicate in high-level abstractions, while formal verification requires discrete symbolic states. We leverage techniques for translating natural language to the probabilistic language of thought to ground the LLM's output in actionable symbolic representations. [SOURCE-24]

2.  **Constraint Database**: This module stores the LTL formulas $\Phi$ representing the safety rules of the environment. These can be static (hard-coded laws of physics) or dynamic (context-dependent rules). 3.  **Runtime Verifier**: Given the mapped propositions from the Intent Translator and the constraints from the database, the Runtime Verifier performs formal model checking. It computes whether the proposed action sequence $\sigma_{prop}$ satisfies $\Phi$. Mathematically, it checks if $\sigma_{prop} \models \phi_{safe}$.

### Verification Algorithm
The verification process functions as a filter. Let $A_{LLM}$ be the action proposed by the LLM at time $t$.

$$
A_{exec} = 
\begin{cases} 
A_{LLM} & \text{if } \text{Verify}(A_{LLM}, \Phi) = \text{True} \\
A_{fallback} & \text{if } \text{Verify}(A_{LLM}, \Phi) = \text{False}
\end{cases}
$$

The $\text{Verify}$ function simulates the immediate future state transitions based on $A_{LLM}$. If any transition leads to a state $s \in \mathcal{S}_{unsafe}$, the action is rejected. The fallback action $A_{fallback}$ is a pre-computed safe action (e.g., "maintain current heading" or "stop") designed to preserve system stability.

## Expected Results

We hypothesize that the Formal Verification Wrapper will significantly outperform the baselines in safety-critical metrics. Specifically, we expect the FVW agent to achieve zero critical safety violations in scenarios where the Vanilla LLM and CoT baselines fail due to hallucinations or probabilistic errors. In terms of task performance, we anticipate a trade-off. The FVW agent may exhibit slightly lower task success rates in "edge cases" where the LLM proposes a risky but necessary action to complete the task, which the conservative formal verifier rejects.  We also expect the latency to be higher than the Vanilla LLM due to the verification step but manageable for real-time control given the efficiency of modern model checkers.

### Limitations
While the FVW offers strong safety guarantees, it is not without limitations. The primary bottleneck is the accuracy of the Intent Translator. If the LLM generates an intent that is mistranslated into symbolic logic, the verification step may validate an incorrect plan, leading to a false sense of security. Additionally, defining comprehensive safety constraints $\Phi$ for complex, open-world environments is difficult; incomplete constraints may leave loopholes for unsafe behavior. The system also relies on the availability of a "safe fallback" action, which may not always exist in all physical scenarios.

### Broader Impact and Ethical Considerations
The integration of formal verification into AI agents represents a significant step toward responsible AI. However, there are ethical considerations regarding responsibility. If a formal verification wrapper is in place, there is a risk that developers may become complacent, assuming the system is infallible. It is crucial to communicate that the safety guarantees are only as good as the formal specifications provided by humans. Furthermore, over-reliance on the fallback mechanism could lead to systems that are overly cautious, potentially refusing to act in emergency situations where a calculated risk is necessary to save lives. Balancing safety with autonomy remains a key challenge for the future of neuro-symbolic AI . [SOURCE-30]

## Conclusion

This paper presented the Formal Verification Wrapper (FVW), a novel neuro-symbolic architecture designed to enforce safety in LLM-based control agents. By mapping the stochastic outputs of Large Language Models to rigorous Temporal Logic formulas, the FVW introduces a necessary layer of System 2 reasoning into the execution loop. Our approach addresses the critical limitations of standard LLMs—specifically their lack of reliability and propensity for hallucination—by leveraging the determinism of symbolic verification.

The proposed architecture not only enhances the safety of autonomous agents in simulation but also provides a framework for explainable and verifiable AI in safety-critical industries. Future work will focus on learning the Intent Translator end-to-end to minimize translation errors and expanding the constraint database to support more complex, multi-agent environments. As we move towards the integration of AI into the fabric of physical society, architectures like the FVW will be essential for bridging the gap between the powerful but unruly capabilities of neural networks and the strict demands of formal safety.
